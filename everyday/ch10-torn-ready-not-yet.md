# Chapter 10: Torn, Ready, Not Yet

---

## The scene

Three coffees, one month, three people you love.

Your friend has spent a year "about to leave" her job, and today
she is running both sides of the argument again, unprompted, in
order. Your brother has decided to buy the house, told you the
number, and is asking about inspectors, and you are still
explaining why the market worries you. And your dad, who cannot
hear the television anymore, waves off the hearing aid
conversation for the fourth time with "we'll see, nothing urgent."

You gave all three the same thing: your good advice, warmly
delivered. It landed three different ways, which is to say it
mostly did not land at all. They were not in the same state, and
the state, not the content, decides what a person can hear.

## What is actually happening

Before anyone can hear your words, they are in one of three
states, and the professionals triage this before choosing a single
sentence.

**Torn** is two good reasons pulling opposite ways. Not
indecision. Real weight on both sides, and shame accumulating on
top, because everyone keeps implying she should be done deciding
by now. Argue for one side and you add pressure to a person
already compressed. The move is to name the tension so accurately
it stops needing to be defended.

**Ready** is decided, waiting on logistics. Persuading a ready
person is mildly insulting, because being sold what you have
already bought implies your deciding did not count. Stop
convincing. Start scheduling.

**Not Yet** is the hardest, because there is no problem to push
against. Your dad does not experience a hearing problem. He
experiences a television problem, occasionally. You cannot sell a
solution into an absence, and every urgent push manufactures the
resistance it fears. The move is to find the future where the
problem exists and let him describe it himself.

Misread the state and the best words in this book fire into the
wrong person. Read it first and half your sentences become
unnecessary.

## The words

For the torn, name the tension and remove the shame:

```
THEY'RE TORN

  "It sounds like you're torn between staying put and
   making a change."
  "It seems like part of you wants to move and part
   wants to wait."
  "It sounds like both options cost you something, just
   in different currencies."
  "It seems like you're not indecisive, you're just
   being honest about the tradeoffs."
```

For the ready, stop selling and start scheduling:

```
THEY'RE READY

  "It sounds like the thinking's done. Now it's just
   logistics."
  "You're not asking if, you're asking how. Let's
   answer that."
  "Let's put a real date on it. Not soon, not this
   week. A date."
```

For the not yet, find the future where the problem lives:

```
NO PROBLEM YET

  "What would have to be true for this to become a
   priority?"
  "If nothing changes in six months, what does that
   look like?"
  "What would need to break first before this became
   worth fixing?"
  "Is this a someday thing, or a never-really thing?"
```

That last line is the bravest sentence in the family, and it
works because it makes "never" a comfortable answer. Most people
will not ask it. They would rather keep a dead hope on the
forecast, and that preference costs more at the kitchen table
than it does on a sales floor.

## What good sounds like

**The torn friend stops defending.** You named both sides
generously and she went quiet, then said "yes, that's exactly
it," and then, for the first time in a year, said something new.
Nobody defends a position you have just described accurately.
The new sentence is the decision starting to move.

**The ready brother hands you a job.** "Can you come to the
inspection?" Take it. That is what support looks like to a ready
person, and it is worth more than your market analysis, which he
will remember you finally dropped.

**Your dad describes the future himself.** "I suppose if I
couldn't hear the grandkids on the phone, that'd be different."
He just built the priority you could not sell him. Do not pounce.
Not-yet timelines are real, and the question's whole job was to
plant the marker he will steer by.

**The state changes mid-conversation.** They do. A torn person
who feels understood can turn ready in ten minutes, and your job
changes with them: stop naming the tension and hand them a date.

## When not to do this

Do not diagnose out loud like a clinician. "You're clearly torn"
delivered as a verdict is a label pointed wrong, and the whole
apparatus collapses into a personality quiz. The state guides
which words you choose. It is not itself content.

Do not weaponize the not-yet questions to manufacture urgency
you merely wish existed. "What would have to break first"
asked honestly is a gift. Asked as a sales move on someone with
no real problem, it is the loss-move from Chapter 5, and family
tables have long memories for it.

And check your own state before you check anyone else's. Half
the bad advice at those three coffees came from you being torn
about their decisions. The triage runs on the advisor too.

## The bigger room

A sales manager reads a forecast: eleven deals, eleven names,
and underneath them, four torn buyers being pressured, five
not-yets being chased with discounts they have no problem to
spend on, and two ready buyers being actively talked back out of
their decision with continued selling. The same three boxes, word for
word, sit in the professional version, on the page a manager pins
up. The states are not a home technique scaled up or a work
technique scaled down. They are how people decide, at every table, at
every size.

## Try it this week

Three names, one line each. The three people you are currently
trying to move on something. Write the state next to each,
honestly, with one piece of evidence. Then notice which of your
recent sentences to each were aimed at the wrong state, and say
one right-state sentence this week instead. The advice you stop
giving will do as much work as the sentence you start saying.
