# Chapter 5: The Big Purchase

---

## The scene

Hour three at the dealership. You came in for the certified pre-owned
you found online, and since then you have met the sticker price, the
four-square worksheet, a monthly payment that hides the real number,
and a sales manager who exists mainly as a reason to leave the room.
Someone else, you are told, is coming to look at the same car this
afternoon.

You are tired, which is part of the design. You are also about to pay
more than you needed to, not because anyone lied to you, but because
every number in that building landed before yours did, and every
number that lands first bends the ones that follow.

The big purchase, a car, a kitchen, a roof, a wedding venue, is the
one room in this book where the other side negotiates for a living
and you do it every few years. You will not out-rep them. You do not
need to. You need four tools, and you already have three of them.

## What is actually happening

The sticker is an anchor. Its job is not to be paid. Its job is to
make the next number feel reasonable, and it works even when you know
what it is doing, which is why your defense is not willpower. It is a
question. A number that gets probed has to explain itself, and
numbers built on air explain themselves badly.

The monthly payment is a frame. It exists to make the total
invisible. The urgency is mostly atmosphere, Chapter 4's borrowed
clocks in a louder costume. And the someone-else-this-afternoon line
is a loss move, built to make doing nothing feel expensive. Some of
these pressures are real some of the time. The whole game is finding
out which, and the finding out is done with the same probes, ranges,
trades, and silence the professionals use on each other.

One rule above all of it. **Your number goes down once, flat, and
then you are quiet.** You do not defend it. You do not explain it.
The moment you argue for your number, you have told them it is
negotiable with you, and they only need one of you to negotiate it.

## The words

Their number explains itself, or it moves:

```
PROBING THEIR NUMBER

  "Where did that number actually come from?"
  "Compared to what, exactly?"
  "Is that the number, or just the first one anyone
   said out loud?"
  "What would have to change for that number to move?"
```

Before any exact number, a range:

```
GETTING A RANGE

  "Before we talk exact numbers, what range are you
   even considering?"
  "Most deals like this land between two numbers.
   Where do you see it?"
  "What's the low end you'd still feel good about?"
```

Money is one currency in the room, not the only one:

```
FINDING THE OTHER CURRENCIES

  "If not the price, what else is actually on the table?"
  "If we can't move the number, what else could move?"
  "Is there a non-cash way to make this work for both
   of us?"
```

And your own number, with its stage directions from the catalog:

```
YOUR NUMBER

  [Say the number, then let the room go quiet. Don't
   soften it after.]
  [Count to five in your head before you say anything
   else.]
  Whoever speaks next usually gives up ground. Let it
   be them.
```

Keep the currencies separate on a big purchase. The car price, the
trade-in, the financing, and the add-ons are four negotiations, and
the worksheet that shows you all four at once exists so a loss on
one can hide inside a win on another. One at a time, in writing,
starting with the price of the thing itself.

## What good sounds like

**The number moves without yours moving.** You probed, you went
quiet, and a new figure appeared. Note what just happened: you paid
nothing for that movement. Probe again.

**A mechanism.** "End of quarter is real for us, the floor plan
interest on that car started in March." Real information, offered to
explain a number. Now you know their clock, which is worth more
than a discount.

**"Let me talk to my manager."** Chapter 4 again: you are talking to
a messenger. Send something worth carrying. "What would your manager
need to see to say yes to my number?" hands the messenger a job
instead of a stall.

**The pressure lines, recognized.** "What are you already losing by
not deciding right now?" and its cousins are shipped loss moves, and
now you can hear the shape of them coming. The counter is the
deadline test you already own: "What actually breaks if this takes
one more week?" If the answer is a shrug, the afternoon buyer is
atmosphere.

## When not to do this

Know your exit before you walk in, or none of this is negotiation.
A buyer who cannot walk away is a buyer doing theater, and the
other side reads it in minutes. The number you will not cross and
the alternative you actually have get decided at your kitchen
table, not under the fluorescent lights.

Do not bluff a competing quote you do not have. Same rule as the
raise chapter, same reason. Discovered once, it reprices everything
you say after.

And ease off the full toolkit on a private seller who priced the
thing fairly. Grinding a fair number because the tools work is how
you win four hundred dollars and feel wrong about it in the
driveway. The fairness standard from this series applies to you
too: if the deal only works for one of us, it does not actually
work.

## The bigger room

An acquisition team opens diligence on a company whose founder wants
nine figures. The first number on the table is an anchor built by
bankers to do exactly what the sticker does. The acquirer's lead
does not counter it. She probes it: "What's the story behind that
specific figure?" Then the range, then the currencies that are not
price, earn-outs, retention packages, timing, exactly the trades
from the box above with more zeros attached. Then her number, once,
flat, and the longest silence anyone in that conference room can
remember.

The corporate manual's anchoring chapter teaches her half of that
table. You now hold the other half, and they are the same half.

## Try it this week

You do not need a car. The next quote that crosses your kitchen
table, the plumber, the insurance renewal, the gym's annual rate,
gets one probe, one range question, and one five-second silence
after your counter. Small rooms are where the reflexes get built,
which is the entire argument of this book.
