Chapter 4: The Raise

The scene

You have been doing the bigger job for a year. The title never caught
up, the money never caught up, and everyone involved quietly knows it.
You have rehearsed the conversation in the shower so many times you
have a favorite version.

The real version goes differently. You catch your manager at the end
of a one-on-one, your voice does something apologetic, and you say
you were "hoping we could maybe talk about compensation at some
point." They say, warmly, "let me see what I can do." You say thanks.
Walking back to your desk you realize you asked for nothing, got
nothing, and feel grateful anyway.

Nothing about that exchange was a negotiation, and that is the
problem. This chapter is the version where one actually happens.

What is actually happening

Asking someone with power over you scrambles the tools you have been
using since Chapter 1, because two feelings are pricing your ask
before you open your mouth. Gratitude, which whispers that asking is
disloyal. And fear, which whispers that asking has a price. Both push
you toward the hallway mumble, and the hallway mumble is designed,
unconsciously, to not count as asking.

Meanwhile the negotiation started without you, which you know from
Chapter 1. There is a comp cycle. There is a budget your manager does
not control. There is a person above them who will actually decide.
Your manager is often not your counterpart. They are your messenger,
and half of this conversation is finding out what the messenger needs
to carry the message well.

So the shape of the ask: name the awkwardness before it names you,
put down a number built on evidence, find out where the decision
actually lives, and convert every vague answer into a mechanism and
a date. All four moves are tools you already have.

The words

Getting the meeting, built so "no" is easy to say and easy to
survive:

Getting The Meeting.

Quote. Is now a bad time to talk numbers? End quote.

Quote. Is it unreasonable to ask for a decision? End quote.

Quote. Would it be difficult to answer by Friday? End quote.

Opening it, with the awkwardness named first:

Going First On The Awkward Part.

Quote. It might seem like I'm overstepping something that isn't mine to touch. End quote.

Quote. You probably think I'm just trying to squeeze you here. End quote.

Finding out where the decision actually lives:

Locating The Decision.

Quote. It seems like this needs a yes from someone else in the room first. End quote.

Quote. It seems like there's a number in mind, even if it hasn't been said yet. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like the timeline matters more to you than the price does. End quote.

Converting the vague answer:

Quote. WHEN THE ANSWER IS "LET ME SEE WHAT I CAN DO. End quote.

Quote. What would need to happen for this to be an easy yes? End quote.

Quote. What would need to be true for this to work? End quote.

Quote. How did you land on that number? End quote.

Quote. Where did that number actually come from? End quote.

Your own number goes down flat and once, with its evidence attached.
Market data, the scope you carry now, the number, no apology stapled
to any of it. Then the Chapter 2 discipline: quiet. The first person
to rescue the silence usually pays for it, and it does not have to be
you.

What good sounds like

A mechanism and a date. "Comp reviews close in March, I can take
this in at the director level next week." That is a real answer. You
now know the room, the calendar, and your messenger's next move.
Write it down in front of them, kindly. Things written down in front
of people happen more often.

A correction with information in it. "It's not really about
budget, it's that the band tops out." You just learned the actual
constraint, which means the actual negotiation can start. Band
problems have band solutions, title, scope, timing, and now you are
negotiating the right thing.

"Let me see what I can do," again. Warm nothing. Do not accept it
twice. One calibrated question, gently: "What would need to happen
for this to be an easy yes?" If the answer is still fog, ask for the
date instead of the raise. A vague yes with no date is a no that is
being polite.

"You're right to ask." Note what is missing from that sentence.
A number. Appreciation without a mechanism is the cheapest currency
in any building. Take it politely and keep locating.

When not to do this

Without the homework, none of this works. The tools in this book
move the leverage you have. They do not manufacture leverage you do
not. If you have no market number and no account of the scope you
carry, get those first. That is a week of work, and it is worth more
than any script in this chapter.

Do not bluff an outside offer you do not have. It is the one move in
the raise conversation that, discovered, converts your manager from
messenger into adversary permanently.

And do not run this in an ambush. Power asks need scheduled rooms.
The no-oriented meeting ask exists exactly so the conversation starts
on legitimate footing instead of in a doorway.

The bigger room

A vendor has to take a twelve percent price increase to their biggest
client. Same scramble: gratitude toward the account, fear of losing
it, a counterpart who is actually a messenger to a committee. The rep
who lands it runs your raise conversation move for move. The audit
first: "You probably think I'm just trying to squeeze you here." The
evidence anchor, once, flat. The locating label: "It seems like this
needs a yes from someone else in the room first." Then the calibrated
conversion of every soft answer into a mechanism and a date.

The corporate manual gives that scene its own chapter in Part V. The
only difference between it and your March conversation is who is
holding the number.

Try it this week

Two sentences on paper before any meeting. The evidence anchor: your
number and the two strongest facts under it, no apology in the
sentence. And the meeting ask, no-oriented, from the box above. Book
the meeting with the second sentence. You have now done more actual
negotiating than the hallway version manages in a year.
