## Page 3: Introduction

> You already negotiate. You did it three times today.
>
> The argument that was not about the dishes. The contractor's
> overage. The meeting you could not decline, the fee you paid
> rather than question, the raise you are still rehearsing in the
> shower. None of it felt like negotiation, because negotiation is
> supposed to happen in glass conference rooms, run by people with
> cufflinks and leverage.
>
> Here is the working premise of this book, learned across a
> thousand live sessions with ordinary people: the glass rooms run
> on about forty sentences, the sentences are short, and they work
> exactly as well at a kitchen table as they do across a
> seven-figure contract. The stakes change. The rooms change. The
> words do not.
>
> Every chapter takes one situation from your actual life, an
> argument, a bill, a raise, a no you owe somebody, and hands you
> the professional tools for it, word for word, from the same
> catalog our commercial clients drill. Every chapter ends in the
> bigger room, the same tool with more zeros attached, so you can
> watch the claim in this book's title earn itself, chapter after
> chapter, until the last one turns it inward.
>
> Two promises about what this book will not do. It will not teach
> you to handle people. Every tool in here works in the open, says
> the true thing, and leaves the other person feeling heard rather
> than played, and the one chapter where misuse is tempting says so
> at length. And it will not pretend reading is practice. Each
> chapter ends with one small rep, with a real person, this week.
> The reps are the book.

## Page 4: How to use this book

> Read Chapters 1 and 2 first, in order. They are the two tools
> every later chapter assumes: naming what the other person feels,
> and listening without assembling your answer.
>
> After that, come in through your own front door. The argument
> you keep having is Chapter 1. The raise is Chapter 4, the car is
> Chapter 5, the bill is Chapter 6, the no you have been
> softening is Chapter 9. The person you cannot seem to move is
> Chapter 10, and it will tell you why.
>
> The script boxes are meant to be used as printed. They read
> strangely out loud at first. Say them anyway. The strangeness
> fades around the sixth repetition, which is a real number from
> real training floors.
>
> And do the try-it-this-week reps small. The tools install on low
> stakes and are simply available afterwards on high ones, which
> is the entire design of this book.

## Page 5: The standard

> These tools work on everyone, including people who trust you.
> That fact is the reason this page exists rather than a reason to
> be quiet about it.
>
> **The tools in this book are used to create clarity, not
> confusion. More dignity, not less. To listen to understand, not
> to win. When the conversation ends, the other side should not
> feel beaten. They should feel heard. That is the standard.**
>
> At home the standard has teeth the office never needed: the
> people across your table are the ones you keep. A won argument
> that leaves your sister feeling handled is a loss with a trophy.
> Any technique that requires the other side not to notice is
> outside this standard, and if you find one in here, it is an
> error and should be reported.
