# Chapter 3: The Repair

---

## The scene

It has been three days since the blowup with your sister about your
parents' care, and neither of you has called.

You have run the argument back a dozen times, and every rerun ends with
you winning it a little harder. She said you only show up when it is
convenient. You said she likes being the martyr. Both of you were
armed with three years of receipts. Now there is silence, and the
longer it holds, the more the silence becomes the argument.

You know somebody has to go first. You also know how going first
usually goes: you call, you explain your side more calmly, she hears
the same case at a lower volume, and round two starts politely. There
is a better version of going first, and it is the one professionals
use when a deal has blown up and somebody has to walk back into the
room. You open with their case against you, not yours against them.

## What is actually happening

After a rupture, the other person is holding an indictment of you. As
long as it stays unsaid, it runs the relationship from under the
table, same as the unnamed feeling in Chapter 1. Every word you say
gets filtered through it. Your explanation sounds like excuses. Your
warmth sounds like strategy. You cannot talk over an indictment. You
can only take it out of their hands by reading it out loud yourself.

Negotiators call this the accusation audit, and it works for one
reason. Saying the worst thing yourself costs it its power, because an
accusation loses most of its charge when the accused agrees it exists.
You are not confessing. You are not even agreeing it is true. You are
saying, accurately, that you know what it looks like from where they
stand. At home, that sentence is usually the whole bridge.

Then you make their side make sense out loud, you ask one question
about what would actually help, and you stop. No case. No receipts.
The moment you append your defense, the repair converts back into
round two, and they will hear that conversion faster than you do.

## The words

Going first, at your own expense:

```
THE AUDIT, their case against you, said by you

  "It might seem like I don't care how this affects you."
  "Sounds like I've offended you."
  "Sounds like you don't trust me."
  "It probably looks like I'm only here for what I can
   get out of this."
```

Making their side make sense, without conceding the argument:

```
MAKING IT MAKE SENSE

  "You've clearly been carrying this longer than just
   today's conversation."
  "That's not an overreaction. That's what this
   situation calls for."
  "Anyone in your position would be asking the same
   questions right now."
  "It's fair to be careful here. The last version of
   this didn't go well."
```

Rebuilding, one question at a time:

```
THE TRUST QUESTIONS

  "At what point did trust break down here?"
  "What's the smallest thing I could do right now that
   would mean something?"
  "What would need to be different this time for it to
   actually be different?"
  "Who let you down the last time something like this
   came up?"
```

And for the moment when it is genuinely stuck:

```
  "It looks like there's nothing I can say to change
   your mind."
```

That last one is not surrender. Said flatly and honestly, it is the
sentence that most often gets answered with "that's not true," which
is the door reopening from their side.

## What good sounds like

**They correct your audit downward.** "I don't think you don't care, I
think you don't notice." You have just traded a three-day indictment
for a specific, workable complaint. Take it. Do not litigate the
difference.

**They answer the smallest-thing question with something small.**
"Just call Mom on Thursdays." People rebuild trust in concrete
inches, not in summit meetings. The small ask is the real one. Do it,
and do it boringly, on schedule, because the schedule is the repair.

**They bring up their own share.** Going first at your expense makes
it safe to own things. If you take your share cleanly and without
celebration, theirs usually follows. If you pounce on it, it retreats.

**"You're right."** Same warning as Chapter 1. After a rupture it
often means "I want this conversation over." Do not bank it. Ask the
smallest-thing question and let the answer tell you where you stand.

## When not to do this

If you actually did the thing, apologize first, plainly, before any
tool. "I said something cruel and I'm sorry" is not a technique and
must never sound like one. The audit comes after the apology, not
instead of it. An audit used to skip accountability is the most
expensive misuse in this book, because the other person will
eventually see the shape of it, and then every future repair reads as
management.

Do not run a repair while you are still keeping score. If part of you
is going first to be the bigger person on record, wait a day.
That motive leaks, and it converts the whole conversation into a
performance review.

And respect the clock. Some people need the three days. Going first is
a door you open, not a door you drag someone through.

## The bigger room

A vendor blew a rollout. Six weeks late, two escalations, and the
renewal meeting is in ten days with a client who has stopped returning
calls. The rep who saves that account does not open with the improved
delivery plan. They open with the audit: "You might be thinking I'll
disappear the second this is signed." Then the trust questions, the
same ones from the box above, word for word: "What would proof look
like here, if my word alone isn't enough?" "What's the smallest thing
I could do right now that would mean something?" The client names
something small and specific. The rep does it, boringly, on schedule.
The renewal conversation that follows is a different conversation.

That is your call to your sister with a purchase order attached. The
corporate manual runs this same sequence across three chapters of its
Disarm stage, and neither version works without the discipline
underneath: go first, at your own expense, and do not defend.

## Try it this week

One repair, the smallest one you have. Not the sister-sized rupture.
The colleague you cut off in a meeting, the friend whose text sat for
a week. One audit line, one make-it-make-sense line, one trust
question, then quiet. Notice which part was hardest to say without
adding "but." That is the muscle this chapter trains, and it is the
same muscle at every size.
