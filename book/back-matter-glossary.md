# Appendix C: Glossary

*Every term this manual uses as a term, in one place, with the chapter that
teaches it. A method with a vocabulary is a method. A method without one is a
set of opinions.*

Definitions shared with *Six Before Yes* and *Same Words, Bigger Rooms* are
carried here word for word. A reader who owns two of these books and finds
them disagreeing is the worst outcome available, so where a term appears in
more than one, the wording is identical and only the chapter tag changes.

---

**Accusation audit.** Saying out loud, before you ask for anything, the worst
things the other side is privately thinking about you. Named first, an
objection loses most of its force. Held back, it gets deployed against you
later, on their timing. Ch 10.

**Anchor.** The first number said out loud, and the reference point every
later number is measured against, including by people who know that is
happening. Test one before you counter it. Ch 19.

**Asking label.** An observation used instead of a question, when the question
itself would reveal what you are worried about. *"It sounds like you're
comparing this against something else you're weighing."* Ch 16.

**Attack, the three kinds.** Every attack in a negotiation is one of three
things: they are protecting themselves, they do not feel heard, or they are
using it deliberately as a tactic. The three look nearly identical from the
outside and take different responses, which is why the read comes before the
reply. Ch 14.

**BATNA / walkaway / exit.** What you do if this deal does not happen. It does
not have to be good. It has to exist and you have to have priced it, because
desperation is the one leak no technique closes. Ch 2, and absent by
definition in Ch 29.

**Blocker.** Someone who cannot say yes but can make yes impossible. Usually
earned that power by being burned before. Ch 9, Ch 27.

**Bracket.** A range rather than a number. People who will not give you a
figure will give you a range, and the top of a volunteered range is almost
always above the number they would have named. Ch 19.

**Calibrated question.** An open question, built on *how* or *what*, that
hands the problem to the other side. Never *why*, which is heard as an
accusation. *"How am I supposed to do that?"* Ch 15.

**Close.** Stage 4 of the Protocol. Resolution through restraint rather than
pressure. Ch 21 to Ch 25.

**Concession after silence.** A rep improving their own offer with nobody
having asked. The single most expensive habit on a sales floor, trivially
countable off a recording, and the number that pays for a rollout. Ch 8,
Ch 31, Ch 33.

**Decoy objection.** The stated obstacle that is not the real one. Recognized
by surviving every solution you offer it. Ch 11.

**Disarm.** Stage 2 of the Protocol. Saying the objection out loud before they
can, so it cannot be deployed against you later. Ch 10 to Ch 14.

**Fairness standard.** A permanent right, handed to the other side early and
honored when used, to stop you the moment something stops feeling fair. It
buys the thing every closing conversation is short of: a counterpart who tells
you when something is wrong instead of going quiet and deciding later. Ch 23.

**Field card.** A one-page compression of a tool: trigger, words, what to
listen for, the metric, the failure mode. Thirty-three of them, one per
chapter, collected in Appendix A.

**Label.** A short declarative sentence that names the other side's emotional
state out loud, offered without a question mark and without a defense. The
first tool in the Protocol and the one everything else is built on. Ch 6.

**Leverage, three kinds.** *Positive*, you have something they want.
*Negative*, you can cost them something they fear losing, usually the
strongest and best left unsaid. *Normative*, their own stated standards held
against an inconsistency in their position. Ch 2, and unavailable in the form
Ch 29 describes.

**Mandate.** What you are actually authorized to do: objective, floor, trade
space, escalation line. Four numbers and a sentence. Anything vaguer is a
mood.

**Mirror.** The last one to three words the other side said, repeated back
with a slight upward inflection, followed by nothing. The whole tool. Ch 7.

**Mislabel.** A deliberately slightly wrong statement, offered to draw a
correction. The correction is the diagnosis. Ch 9.

**Negative label.** Naming the unpleasant thing that is present in the room
and has not been said. Distinct from the accusation audit, which is
pre-emptive: this one is a response to what is already in the air. Ch 11.

**No-oriented question.** A question built so that *no* is the comfortable
answer, because people agree to things they are free to refuse. *"Have you
given up on getting this sorted?"* Ch 12, Ch 21.

**Position.** What someone asks for. Distinct from the stake, which is why
they need to ask.

**Protocol, the.** Read, Disarm, Steer, Close. The four stages you run inside
a conversation, in that order, every time. Ch 2, and the spine of the manual.

**Read.** Stage 1 of the Protocol. Diagnosis before any ask. Ch 4 to Ch 9.

**Reframe.** A change to what the negotiation is about, without any change to
what is on the table. Ch 24.

**Rollout.** The four-week sequence that converts a team who has read this
manual into a team who runs it under pressure. Not a training event. Ch 31.

**Scorecard.** A twelve-row instrument for auditing a recorded negotiation
against the four stages. Scores the call, never the person. Ch 33, blank copy
in Appendix B.

**Stake.** What a party actually stands to gain or lose, including the parts
nobody says out loud. Respond to this, not to the position.

**State.** Torn, Ready, or Not Yet. What someone is right now rather than who
they are permanently. States change inside a single meeting, and producing
that change is the job. Ch 5.

**Steer.** Stage 3 of the Protocol. Moving them without pressure. Ch 15 to
Ch 20.

**Stop.** The number where you walk, written down before contact. Written,
because a stop that lives in your head bends at minute ninety.

**Summary.** A restatement complete enough that the other side confirms it.
Proof of understanding is the price of admission to Stage 4: you cannot close
a person who does not believe you understood what they were closing. Ch 17.

**Sweep.** The pre-signature question that invites the ask they were holding.
*"What's the thing you almost brought up but didn't?"* Cheap now, expensive
later. Ch 25.

**Tactical silence.** A pause used as an instrument rather than suffered as a
gap. Silence is not empty. It is still your turn, and you have not used it
yet. Ch 8.

**Talk-time ratio.** The share of a call you spoke. Guessed at around 50% by
experienced operators and measured near 70%. The headline number of a rollout
and the one to report upward. Ch 4, Ch 31.

**"That's right."** The one reliable green light. Distinct from *"you're
right,"* which is appeasement and the sound of someone ending a conversation.
Ch 22.

**Trade.** A concession with something named in the same breath as coming
back. Nothing leaves your side of the table for free, because a free
concession teaches them that pressure works. Ch 18.

**Validation.** Telling the other side that their position makes sense given
where they are standing. Concedes nothing, because understanding a position is
not agreeing with it. Ch 13.

## The three conditions

The named exceptions, where the Protocol stops applying. Ch 30.

**Bad faith.** They are using the process for something other than reaching a
deal. Tested by asking for a small, cheap commitment, which costs them
optionality and which they will not give.

**No authority.** Your counterpart is genuine, engaged, and structurally
unable to say yes. Tested by asking for a decision with a date: someone with
authority gives you one or explains the mechanism, someone without gives you a
feeling.

**Genuine deadlock.** Both constraints are real and no trade satisfies both.
The rarest of the three and the most often misdiagnosed. Tested by probing and
bracketing the constraint, since a real one survives being tested.

## Terms this manual deliberately does not use

**Personality type.** States are weather, not climate. A type is a claim about
who someone is permanently, which is unfalsifiable in a meeting and useless in
the next ten minutes. Ch 5 replaces it with the three states.

**Win-win.** Describes an outcome, prescribes no behavior, and is most often
said by the party who is winning.

**Closing technique.** Nothing in Part IV is a technique for extracting a yes.
Stage 4 is restraint, and the chapter that would carry the pressure moves is
Ch 30, which tells you to stop.
