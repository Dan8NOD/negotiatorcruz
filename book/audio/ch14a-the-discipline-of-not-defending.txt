The Standard

An attack in a negotiation is exactly one of three things, and you cannot
respond well until you know which one you are looking at.

Either they are protecting themselves and lashing out because they feel
exposed. Or they don't feel heard, and volume is the only lever they have
left. Or they are using the attack deliberately as a tactic, to knock you
off balance and see what falls out.

The three look nearly identical from the outside. All three raise the
temperature, all three sound personal, and all three trigger the same instinct
in you, defend, or change the subject. Both instincts are wrong, and which one
is more wrong depends on which of the three you're actually facing. That is
the problem this tool solves.

The Attack Decoder does not require you to guess. It gives you a single move
that identifies the type and begins defusing it at the same time, which matters
because you will not get a quiet moment mid-attack to run a diagnosis. You get
one sentence, under pressure, and it has to do both jobs at once.

When to reach for it

Use the decoder when:
The temperature jumps and you don't know why. The most common trigger.
 Something you said landed differently than you intended and you have no read
 on what.
The attack is personal rather than about terms. "This is a joke" is not a
 position on price. It is one of the three, and you need to know which.
You're about to defend yourself. Treat the defensive impulse as the
 trigger for the tool. If you feel the urge to justify, decode instead.
Someone has escalated in front of an audience, their team, your team, a
 committee. Public attacks skew heavily toward the third type, and the decoder
 is the cheapest way to find out without accusing anyone of gamesmanship.

Do not reach for it when:
The complaint is specific and legitimate. "You missed the delivery date
 twice" is not an attack, it is information. Decoding a real grievance reads as
 deflection and will earn you a second, worse one.
You already know the type. If they've told you they feel ignored, you
 don't need a diagnostic, go straight to the response.
You are the one who is angry. Every line in this chapter delivered by
 someone managing their own temper comes out as sarcasm. Use silence until your
 state is fixed. This is not optional and it is the most common way the tool
 gets misfired.
It is the third attack in the same conversation. Repeated escalation with
 no movement is a signal about the deal, not a problem to be labeled away. See
 Chapter 30.

The words

The decoder works because a well-chosen negative label is a test. You name
the worst plausible reading of the situation out loud, and their reaction sorts
the three types for you.

Aim slightly wrong on purpose. A label that is close but not exact draws a
correction, and the correction is the diagnosis.

THE DECODER, pick one, then stop talking

Suspecting DEFENSIVE (they feel exposed)

Quote. It probably seems like we're being selfish. End quote.

Quote. Seems like you think I'm being unreasonable. End quote.

Quote. It probably seems like we don't care about you. End quote.

Suspecting UNHEARD (volume is their last lever)

Quote. It seems like there's something else here that I'm not addressing. End quote.

Quote. Seems like you've got something on your mind. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like you have a reason to say that. End quote.

Type unknown. The safe opener when you have no read

Quote. Seems like something's going on here. End quote.

Quote. It seems like there's something here that bothers you. End quote.

Quote. Sounds like this bothers you. End quote.

The third row is the one to memorize. "Seems like something's going on here" is
almost impossible to deliver badly, commits you to no theory, and works on all
three types.

Once the type is identified, the response diverges:

DEFENSIVE → give them the exit, don't take the ground

Quote. That reaction makes complete sense given what's on the line. End quote.

Quote. Anyone in your position would be asking the same questions right now. End quote.

Quote. That's not an overreaction. That's what this situation calls for. End quote.

UNHEARD → hand back the floor, visibly

Quote. You're doing more processing here than you're getting credit for. End quote.

Quote. You've clearly been carrying this longer than just today's conversation. End quote.

TACTICAL → name the state, decline the fight, hold

Quote. It sounds like you're waiting for me to just accept where this landed. End quote.

Quote. It looks like there's nothing I can say to change your mind. End quote.

Those last two are from the acknowledgement family, and they are deliberately
uncomfortable. A tactical attack depends on you scrambling. An opponent who
calmly describes the tactic back to them, and then says nothing, has removed
the only thing the tactic was going to produce.

Stay in the moment. The strong instinct mid-attack is to change the subject,
move to logistics, or offer something. All three confirm the attack worked.

What good sounds like

A correction. "It's not that I think you're unreasonable, it's that I've
been asking for this for three weeks." That is the diagnosis, volunteered, in
one sentence, and it is the unheard type. You now know exactly what to do.
Corrections are the most common good outcome and the reason to aim slightly
wrong.

A drop in volume with no words. Usually defensive. The label gave them
somewhere to put it and they no longer need the volume. Do not fill the silence.

"Exactly" or "that's right." You landed it. Go to the matching response
above and then stop.

A tactical attack that simply stops. The clearest tell of type three. A
deliberate tactic that gets described out loud and met with silence has nowhere
to go, and it will often be abandoned without acknowledgement. The conversation
just resumes at a normal temperature. Do not point this out. Ever.

What is not good: escalation after an accurate label. That usually means
you delivered it with an edge. The words were right and 7-38-55 was against you.
