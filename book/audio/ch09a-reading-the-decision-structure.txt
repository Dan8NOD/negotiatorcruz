The Standard

The person in front of you is frequently not the person you are negotiating
with.

They are the person who was sent. Behind them is a finance stakeholder with a
number in their head, an operations lead who had a bad quarter with a vendor
that wasn't you, someone whose reputation is attached to championing this, and
someone whose reputation is attached to having argued against it. You will
likely meet none of them. All of them will vote.

Reading the decision structure means establishing, before you make any ask,
who can actually say yes - and what has to be true for them to. Chapter 1
established that the negotiation started without you. This chapter is the
instrument for finding out who was in the room.

The failure this prevents is specific and it is the most expensive one in the
book: a perfectly executed negotiation with someone who was never able to
agree. Every stage run correctly, every tool landed, and it dies in a meeting
you weren't invited to, for reasons nobody will tell you.

When to reach for it

Reach for it:
Before your first substantive ask. Always. This is not an advanced move
 saved for complex deals; it is the price of entry.
When the process slows down for no stated reason. Unexplained delay is
 almost always structural, not attitudinal. Somebody upstream has a question
 nobody has passed to you.
When your contact keeps agreeing with you. A counterpart who agrees with
 everything and commits to nothing is usually a person with no authority being
 polite about it. Enthusiasm is not a proxy for power and they are frequently
 inversely correlated.
When the pronouns change. See below. This is the highest-value signal in
 the chapter and it costs nothing to monitor.

Do not reach for it in a way that insults them. "Are you the decision
maker?" is the crudest possible version of this question, it is faintly
humiliating to answer, and the honest answer to it is often a lie. Every tool
in this chapter exists to get that information without ever asking that
question.

Pronouns are the cheapest intelligence available

Listen to which word people reach for. They are not managing it, because
nobody thinks pronouns are load-bearing.

They say: "I" - "I'd want to see the security review.". It usually means: Real authority, or at least real ownership. Take it seriously.

They say: "We" - "We'd need to think about the timeline.". It usually means: A committee exists. Find out who's in it. "We" is the most common word in the mouth of someone who cannot decide alone.

They say: "They" - "They'll never approve that.". It usually means: Authority is elsewhere and your contact has already positioned themselves as a bystander. This is either true, or a shield. Both matter.

They say: A name - "Rachel's going to ask about SLAs.". It usually means: A gift. Write it down. Rachel is now the most important person in your deal.

They say: Pronoun switches mid-sentence - "I think we could - well, they'd have to sign off.". It usually means: You just watched someone's actual authority narrow in real time. That sentence is the truest thing they'll say all meeting.

That last row is worth sitting with. People start sentences from the position
they wish they occupied and finish them from the one they actually do. The
switch is where the wish runs out.

The words

Three instruments, escalating in directness.

Information labels - name the gap

Say out loud that something is missing. Do not ask what it is. The distinction
matters: a question puts them in the position of deciding whether to tell you,
while a statement puts them in the position of correcting an inaccuracy, and
people find the second much harder to resist.

Information Labels.

Quote. It seems like there's something I'm not seeing. End quote.

Quote. It seems like there's a detail here nobody's said out loud yet. End quote.

Quote. Looks like this decision depends on something we haven't talked about. End quote.

Quote. Seems like there's a piece of context I'm missing. End quote.

Quote. Seems like there's more here than meets the eye. End quote.

Quote. It seems like I've missed something. End quote.

Quote. It looks like I need help with this. End quote.

Quote. It seems like you have a reason for asking. End quote.

Quote. Seems like you have some steps in mind already. End quote.

Quote. Seems like you have an idea as to what stands in the way. End quote.

Quote. Seems like you have an idea as to how to proceed. End quote.

Quote. It looks like your priorities have changed. End quote.

Quote. It looks like there's no movement on any of these points. End quote.

Quote. It seems like you haven't given this much thought. End quote.

Quote. Looks like no one is going to undermine this deal. End quote.

"It looks like I need help with this" is the most disarming sentence in the
family and it is chronically underused, because it sounds like weakness. It
isn't. It converts you from an adversary into a person with a problem, and
people who would never volunteer information to an adversary will happily
assist someone who is stuck.

Power labels - name the constraint they can't admit

The advanced instrument, and the counterintuitive one. You say out loud that
they lack authority. It sounds insulting. It is, reliably, one of the fastest
ways to find the real decision maker.

Power Labels.

Quote. It sounds like whoever's really in charge isn't even in this room. End quote.

Quote. It feels like the real decision-maker is somewhere else entirely. End quote.

Quote. It seems like you'd need permission just to say yes to this. End quote.

Quote. It feels like someone else is making this decision for you. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like control matters more right now than the actual terms. End quote.

Quote. It seems like your hands are completely tied here. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like you don't actually have a say in any of this. End quote.

Quote. It feels like this was decided before we ever sat down. End quote.

Quote. It seems like you don't have any real room to move on this. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like you're just here to deliver someone else's answer. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like nothing here actually depends on what you think. End quote.

Quote. It feels like you're powerless here. End quote.

There are only two possible responses, and both are wins.

They confirm - "honestly, yeah, this is Rachel's call" - and you now know
the structure, plus you have a counterpart who feels understood rather than
interrogated, which tends to make them an ally in reaching Rachel.

Or they push back - "no, I've got the authority here, I just have to
socialize it" - and people defending their authority overstate it and then
feel obliged to demonstrate it. You have just made it socially expensive for
them to hide behind a committee later.

Handle with care. Deliver these flat and warm, never sharp. "It sounds like
you're just here to deliver someone else's answer" said with any edge at all is
a straightforward insult and will end the relationship. Said neutrally, as an
observation about their situation rather than about them, it lands as empathy - 
because that's what it is. Being the person sent to deliver someone else's
answer is genuinely unpleasant, and almost nobody acknowledges it.

The strongest of the twelve is "It sounds like control matters more right now
than the actual terms." That one is worth deploying whenever a negotiation has
become strangely rigid over something small. It is very often correct, and it
is almost never said.

Mislabels - be deliberately wrong

The precision instrument. State something you believe to be false, calmly, as
though you believe it. The urge to correct an inaccuracy is stronger than the
urge to volunteer a truth, and this tool runs entirely on that asymmetry.

MISLABELS - decision structure

Quote. It looks like this decision is entirely yours to make. End quote.

Quote. It seems like your team is fully on board with all of this. End quote.

Quote. It looks like everyone on your side agrees on everything. End quote.

Quote. It seems like timing isn't really an issue on your end. End quote.

Quote. It looks like there's nothing left to negotiate here. End quote.

MISLABELS - position

Quote. It seems like price is the only thing standing in the way here. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like you're completely happy with how this is going. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like none of this actually matters to you. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like you've already decided to walk away from this. End quote.

Quote. It seems like you trust this completely already. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like the deadline was never actually real. End quote.

Quote. It seems like you'd sign this without reading it. End quote.

"It looks like this decision is entirely yours to make" is the single most
efficient sentence in this chapter. Nobody who lacks authority lets that stand.
The correction arrives immediately and it usually arrives with names attached.

One rule: a mislabel must be plausible. Absurdly wrong statements get read
as sarcasm and cost you trust. The tool works when they think you genuinely
misunderstood - the correction is generous, not defensive. Aim just slightly
past what you actually believe.

What good sounds like

A name you didn't have. "Rachel's going to want to see the SLAs." The
single most valuable output of this chapter. Write it down exactly, including
whether they used a first name (peer or junior) or a title (senior, or feared).

A number of people. "It'd go to the steering group." Now ask how many and
who chairs it - those are logistics questions and people answer logistics
questions freely, having just declined to answer an authority question.

A confession of limits, with relief in it. People generally dislike
pretending to authority they lack. Power labels give them permission to stop,
and the drop in tension is usually audible.

"Let me bring someone else in." Best possible outcome. You have widened the
room without asking to.

What good does not sound like: "yeah, I can make that call." Delivered
quickly and without detail, that is very often the sentence of someone who
cannot. Real authority tends to come with conditions attached - "I can approve
up to fifty, above that it's Rachel" - because people who genuinely hold
authority know precisely where it stops.
