Chapter 29: Negotiating Internally: Up, Down, and Sideways

Part V · APPLICATIONS

The Standard

You cannot walk away from your own organization.

That single fact changes everything. Chapter 2's walkaway power. The strongest
lever in commercial negotiation, is simply unavailable when the counterpart is
your own manager, your peer in operations, or the person who runs the team you
depend on. There is no BATNA. You will be in a meeting with them again on
Thursday, and again next quarter, and the relationship outlives every
individual negotiation inside it.

So the standard inverts: internally, you optimize for the relationship over
the outcome, because the relationship is the mechanism that produces every
future outcome. A win extracted from a colleague who now avoids you is not a
win. It is a loan against your next six requests.

What survives from the rest of the manual is most of it. Read, Disarm, Steer,
Close all work internally and work well. What changes is the leverage
available, the time horizon, and the fact that both of you are being observed
by people whose opinion of you matters.

When to reach for it
Asking for headcount, budget, or priority. The most common internal
 negotiation and the one most often run as a pitch.
Being asked to absorb something. A deadline, a scope change, another
 team's problem.
Cross-functional deadlock. Two teams, both correct, incompatible
 objectives.
Managing up when you disagree. The highest-stakes version, and the one
 where the audit earns its place.

Do not:
Use negative leverage internally. Threatening to escalate, to slip a
 deadline, or to withdraw cooperation works exactly once and is remembered
 permanently.
Win in front of an audience. A colleague defeated in a meeting will
 reverse it afterwards, in a channel you're not in.
Treat it as lower stakes than external work. Most careers are decided by
 internal negotiations and most people have never trained for one.

The words

Managing up. The audit is the tool

Disagreeing with a senior person is the case the accusation audit was built for,
because every objection they'll have is one you can name first.

Upward Audit.

Quote. You're probably thinking I'm asking for this because my team is behind, not because the plan needs it. End quote.

Quote. It might look like I'm protecting my own area rather than solving the actual problem. End quote.

Quote. You probably think I'd say this regardless of the numbers. End quote.

Quote. It might seem like I'm bringing you a problem instead of a decision. End quote.

Then the calibrated question that makes it their problem to solve rather than
your request to grant:

Quote. How would you want me to handle it if both of these land in the same week? End quote.

Quote. What would you need to see to be comfortable with that? End quote.

Quote. What am I not seeing from where you sit? End quote.

"What am I not seeing from where you sit?" is the safest and most productive
sentence available to anyone managing up. It concedes nothing, invites the
senior view, and it very often produces the actual constraint, which is
often something they haven't been able to say publicly.

Sideways. The hardest direction

Peer negotiation has no authority to appeal to and no exit. Trading (Chapter
18) is the core instrument, and the currency is almost never money.

Peer Trades.

Quote. If I take this on, what comes off my plate? End quote.

Quote. What's cheap for you that would be expensive for me? End quote.

Quote. What would you trade timeline for? End quote.

Quote. If we can't both be first, how do you want to decide it? End quote.

Peer Disarm.

Quote. It probably seems like I'm making this your problem. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like this lands on you at the worst possible time. End quote.

Quote. It seems like you've been carrying more of this than anyone's acknowledged. End quote.

That last one is a positive label (Chapter 13) with real work to do. Internal
work is chronically under-acknowledged, and naming it accurately buys more
cooperation than any escalation.

Downward. The trap is authority

You have leverage and using it is usually the wrong move, because compliance
obtained by rank is compliance you have to keep supplying.

Downward.

Quote. What's making this harder than it needs to be? End quote.

Quote. What would you need from me to make that work? End quote.

Quote. What's the part of this you disagree with? End quote.

Quote. If this goes wrong, what's most likely to have caused it? End quote.

"What's the part of this you disagree with?" presupposes disagreement exists,
which it does, and which will otherwise surface as slow execution rather than
as a stated objection.

What good sounds like

A constraint you didn't know about. The most common good outcome of an
upward negotiation, and it usually reframes the request entirely.

A trade you weren't offered. Peers hold currency they never advertise
because nobody ever asks.

Disagreement, stated. From a report, this is the highest-value output in
the section. Silent disagreement executes badly.

"Let me think about it", and then they do. Internal decisions genuinely
take time, and pressing produces a fast no.

What good does not sound like: immediate agreement from a peer with no
trade. That's compliance, it's remembered, and it will be invoiced later.
