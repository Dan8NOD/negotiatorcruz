Part I · READ

The Standard

Most negotiators operate with an emotional vocabulary of about four words:
frustrated, concerned, excited, and upset. Four words to describe every
internal state a human being can arrive at a meeting carrying. It's like trying
to diagnose an engine with a vocabulary of "loud" and "not loud."

This matters because the next stage depends on it. "Frustrated" and "cornered"
sound adjacent and they are not remotely the same problem. A frustrated
counterpart wants the obstacle removed. A cornered one wants their dignity
returned before they will even look at the obstacle. Offer a discount to the
first and you've helped. Offer a discount to the second and you've told them
their pride is for sale, which is a genuinely worse position than where you
started.

Precision in naming is not a soft skill. It is the accuracy of your
instrument. Everything downstream inherits its error.

When to reach for it

You are always doing this. The question is whether you're doing it
deliberately.

Reach for the structured vocabulary when:
Your first instinct is one of the four default words. That's the tell.
 If "frustrated" arrives instantly, it arrived from your habits and not from
 them.
Something shifted and you can't say what. The temperature changed, the
 pace changed, they got shorter. Somatic reads (below) exist for exactly this.
You're about to decide what kind of negotiation this is. Torn, ready, and
 not-yet are three completely different conversations, and getting the triage
 wrong wastes the next forty minutes.

Do not perform the vocabulary at them. This is a diagnostic you run
internally and then use selectively. A counterpart who hears you name four
emotions in six minutes does not think "how perceptive." They think they are
being processed.

The three states

Before you get specific, get the category right. Almost every counterpart, at
almost every moment, is in one of three states - and the Protocol's response to
each is different enough that misreading this is the most consequential error
in Part I.

Torn - two good reasons, pulling opposite ways

Not indecisive. Not stalling. Holding two legitimate considerations at once and
unable to discharge either. The move is not to argue for your side. It is to
name the tension so accurately that they stop defending it.

They'Re Torn.

Quote. It sounds like you're torn between staying put and making a change. End quote.

Quote. It seems like part of you wants to move and part wants to wait. End quote.

Quote. It looks like you're weighing the cost of acting against the cost of staying. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like you can see both sides clearly, which is exactly why this is hard. End quote.

Quote. It seems like there's no wrong answer here, just two different risks. End quote.

Quote. It looks like you're not stuck - you're just holding two good reasons at once. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like one part of you already knows, and the other part isn't ready to say it. End quote.

Quote. It seems like the hesitation isn't about the decision, it's about the timing. End quote.

Quote. It looks like you're weighing who this affects beyond just you. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like both options cost you something, just in different currencies. End quote.

Quote. It seems like you're not indecisive - you're just being honest about the tradeoffs. End quote.

Look at what those last few do. "You're not indecisive - you're being honest
about the tradeoffs" removes the shame from the hesitation, and the shame is
usually what's keeping the hesitation in place. People defend positions they
feel judged for. Nobody defends a position you've just described generously and
accurately.

Ready - the thinking is done, they're waiting on logistics

The most commonly missed state, and the most expensive. A ready buyer who gets
pitched at will cool, because being sold to after you've decided is mildly
insulting. Stop persuading. Start scheduling.

They'Re Ready.

Quote. It sounds like you've already decided - let's make it easy to finish. End quote.

Quote. You're not asking if - you're asking how. Let's answer that. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like the thinking's done. Now it's just logistics. End quote.

Quote. You've done the hard part already. This part's easy. End quote.

Quote. Let's put a real date on it. Not soon, not this week - a date. End quote.

Quote. It seems like you're ready to move - let's not let momentum slip. End quote.

Quote. You've clearly already run the numbers in your head. Let's make it official. End quote.

Quote. It sounds like you're past convincing and into planning. End quote.

Quote. Let's turn 'yes' into a next step before this conversation ends. End quote.

Quote. Let's lock in the details now, while you're this clear on it. End quote.

Quote. You've thought about this more carefully than most people I work with. End quote.

"Let's put a real date on it. Not soon, not this week - a date." That's the
whole close, sitting in Stage 1, available to anyone who noticed the buyer was
already there.

Not Yet - no problem, therefore no negotiation

The hardest state, because there's nothing to push against. They're not
objecting. They're not resisting. They just don't have a problem yet, and you
cannot sell a solution into an absence. The move is to find the future in which
they would have one, and let them describe it.

No Problem Yet.

Quote. What would have to be true for this to become a priority? End quote.

Quote. How would things need to change for you to even consider this? End quote.

Quote. If nothing changes in six months, what does that look like? End quote.

Quote. What's higher priority than this right now, and why? End quote.

Quote. Right now, where does this rank against everything else you're dealing with? End quote.

Quote. If this became urgent tomorrow, what would have caused that? End quote.

Quote. What would need to break first before this became worth fixing? End quote.

Quote. What's missing that would make this feel necessary instead of nice-to-have? End quote.

Quote. How far off does this feel from something you'd actually act on? End quote.

Quote. Is this a someday thing, or a never-really thing? End quote.

That final line is the most useful sentence in the whole family, and it works
because it makes "never" a comfortable answer. Most operators will not ask it - 
they'd rather keep a dead deal on the forecast than hear it die out loud. That
preference costs more than the deal did.

Reading the body

Words are the slowest channel and the most managed. Tone, pace, breathing, and
posture arrive first and are much harder to run through a filter - which is why
a somatic read frequently gets you the truth two minutes before the sentence
does.

SOMATIC READS - say what you saw, plainly

Quote. Your voice just changed - something showed up right there. End quote.

Quote. Your jaw just tightened. That's not agreement. End quote.

Quote. You went quiet right when it got real. Let's go back there. End quote.

Quote. That quick exhale told me more than the last five sentences. End quote.

Quote. Your shoulders just dropped. That wasn't relief. End quote.

Quote. You leaned back the second I said that number. End quote.

Quote. Your hands stopped moving. That's worth noticing. End quote.

Quote. That pause before you answered was longer than the last one. End quote.

Quote. You crossed your arms right after I finished that sentence. End quote.

Quote. Your breathing changed before your words did. End quote.

Quote. That half-smile didn't reach the rest of your face. End quote.

Two rules, and they are not optional.

Say what you observed, not what it means. "Your jaw tightened" is
observable and hard to argue with. "You're angry" is an accusation. The first
invites an explanation; the second invites a denial.

Use these sparingly and never twice in a row. A somatic read is a strong
instrument. Two of them in ninety seconds stops feeling like attentiveness and
starts feeling like surveillance, and once someone believes they're being
watched for tells they will go still and give you nothing for the rest of the
meeting.

On a phone call you lose posture and keep everything else - pace, breath,
pauses, the pitch shift. Three of the eleven lines above work on audio alone,
and reps who work by phone should drill those three until they're automatic.

What good sounds like

"That's right." The gold standard, and Chapter 22 is about earning it
deliberately. Here it just tells you the read was accurate.

A correction with detail attached. "It's not that I'm torn - I've decided,
I just can't get it through procurement before the freeze." You proposed
"torn," they replaced it with the actual state and handed you the real
obstacle. That is the vocabulary working exactly as designed.

The state changes while you watch. Someone in Not Yet who talks through
"what would need to break first" and arrives, out loud, at a thing that's
already breaking, is now Torn. You didn't argue them there. You gave them a
question and got out of the way.

A longer answer than the question deserved. Emotional accuracy opens
people. When a nine-word question produces a ninety-second answer, the read
landed.

What good does not sound like: "Yeah, exactly, exactly." Enthusiastic
agreement without elaboration is usually politeness. Real recognition tends to
slow people down, not speed them up.
