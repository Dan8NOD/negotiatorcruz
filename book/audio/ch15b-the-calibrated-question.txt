Part III · STEER

Part 1 of 2 covers the standard, when to reach for it, the words, and what good sounds like.

The drill

"Rewrite Ten Demands." Twenty minutes, no preparation, works solo.
Each person writes ten demands they actually made this week. Real ones,
 verbatim - "we need the signed order by Friday," "you'll have to come up on
 the price."
Rewrite each as a How or What question.
Read all ten out loud. They will feel unnatural, indirect, and slightly
 weak. That feeling is the trained instinct objecting, and it's wrong - the
 question outperforms the demand in every measured category except how
 decisive it makes you feel.
Then the hard part: for each question, write what you would do if they
 answered "nothing, there's no way." A calibrated question you have no
 follow-up for is a question you'll answer yourself.

Step 4 is the one people skip and it's the one that determines whether the tool
survives contact.

Scored on

Metric: Talk-time ratio. Target: Under 40% through Stage 3. Above it, the rep is presenting, not steering.

Metric: Question grammar. Target: Zero "why" questions. Binary, easily audited from a transcript.

Metric: Open construction. Target: No calibrated question answerable with yes or no.

Metric: Silence after. Target: Full pause held after each question. A question the rep answers themselves scores as zero questions asked.

Metric: Counterpart-generated solutions. Target: Count the proposals that came from their side. This is the number that says whether Stage 3 is working.

That last row is the one worth putting on a wall. A floor running Steer
correctly generates solutions from the buyer's side of the table, and you can
count them off a recording without any judgment call.

Failure mode

Answering your own question. The rep asks "how am I supposed to do that?",
the pause opens, three seconds pass, it feels like thirty - and they fill it:
"…because I suppose we could look at splitting the payments?"

The question is gone. Worse, they have now made the concession the question was
designed to extract, and made it unprompted. This is the most expensive
four-second habit in commercial negotiation and it is entirely mechanical:
nobody trained them to sit still.

What it looks like on tape: the rep's question and the rep's next sentence
separated by under four seconds.

The recovery: stop, and hand it back explicitly.

Quote. I jumped in on my own question there. Let me put it back to you - how would you want to handle it? End quote.

Naming it is better than pretending it didn't happen, because the counterpart
noticed, and the naming demonstrates that the question was real.

The second failure mode: the calibrated question as a disguised demand.
"How am I supposed to make that work when you've moved the deadline twice?"
That is not a question. It is a complaint with a question mark on it, and the
resentment is audible. The test is simple: if you already know the answer you
want, you're not steering - you're arguing with extra steps.
