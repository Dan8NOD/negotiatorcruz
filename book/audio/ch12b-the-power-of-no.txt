Part 1 of 2 covers the standard, when to reach for it, the words, and what good sounds like.

The drill

"Invert the Ask." Fifteen minutes, no preparation, works solo or in a room.
Each person writes ten questions they actually asked this week - real
 ones, from real conversations. Verbatim.
Mark each one yes-oriented or no-oriented. Most people discover
 nine or ten of theirs are yes-oriented.
Rewrite every yes-oriented question as a no-oriented one.
Say all ten out loud. They will sound wrong. That's the point - the
 phrasing is unfamiliar, not incorrect, and the discomfort fades at about
 the sixth repetition.
Pick two and commit to using them tomorrow.

The variant worth running once: take the single most important question in
your current pipeline - the one you're avoiding asking - and write its
no-oriented form. Most people find that the no-oriented version is one they
could actually say out loud, which is the difference between a question that
exists and a question that gets asked.

Scored on

Metric: Question orientation ratio. Target: Percentage of the rep's questions phrased no-oriented. Start by measuring; most floors are near zero.

Metric: Early no obtained. Target: Did the rep create a safe refusal opportunity before the first ask? Binary.

Metric: Survivable asks. Target: Every no-oriented question asked was one the rep could absorb a "yes" to. Any question that would have ended the deal on a literal answer scores as a failure.

Metric: Re-asks. Target: Zero. Asking the same no-oriented question twice is pressure.

Metric: Stall recovery. Target: Of deals stalled over 30 days, how many got a real answer after a no-oriented question. Track quarterly.

That last row is the one to show leadership. Most floors carry a substantial
number of deals that are neither alive nor dead, and this family is the
cheapest instrument available for converting them into one or the other.

Failure mode

Fearing the no you asked for. The rep phrases the question correctly, and
then - because they cannot bear the answer - immediately supplies an escape
route: "Is this a bad time? Because we can absolutely reschedule, or I can
send something over, or honestly whenever suits you is completely fine…"

The question is gone. They have handed the counterpart three exits and asked
for nothing. What began as a tool for producing engagement has become a way of
avoiding a real answer.

What it looks like on tape: the no-oriented question followed within two
seconds by "because" or "or."

The recovery: the question again, alone, and then the silence.

Quote. Sorry - I'll just ask it straight. Is this a bad time? End quote.

The second failure mode: weaponizing it. "Have you given up on this?"
delivered with an edge is a guilt trip, and it works exactly once before the
relationship is worse than it was. The test is whether you would accept "yes"
gracefully. If a genuine yes would make you angry, you are not asking a
question - you are applying pressure with a question mark attached, and
counterparts distinguish between the two instantly.
