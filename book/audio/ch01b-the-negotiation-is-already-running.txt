Part 0 · THE STANDARD

Part 1 of 2 covers the standard, when to reach for it, the words, and what good sounds like.

The drill

"Backdate the Deal." Twenty minutes. Run it in a pipeline review with no
preparation.
Each rep picks three open deals from their own pipeline.
For each one, they write the date they think the negotiation started, not
 the date of first contact, the date the other side first formed a position.
Next to it: the evidence for that date. A comment, a timing coincidence,
 a budget cycle, a reorganization. No evidence, no date.
Then they list everyone inside the buyer's organization who has an opinion
 about this deal, including people they've never met, and mark which ones
 they can name.
Around the table. The question for each rep is the same one: what happened
 between that date and today that you didn't see?

The output isn't a plan. It's the number of blanks. Most reps discover they can
name one or two participants in a deal with five or six, and that the
negotiation predates their first call by six weeks. That discovery is the
entire point of the drill, and it doesn't survive being told. It has to be
found.

Run this once a quarter on live pipeline, not on closed deals. Closed deals
teach you what you already got right.

Scored on

Auditable from a call recording or a CRM record. No judgment call required.

Metric: Named participants. Target: Rep can name three or more people inside the buyer's organization with an opinion on the deal, and their role in it. Two or fewer means the deal is being run blind.

Metric: Start date accuracy. Target: Rep's stated negotiation start date precedes first contact. If it doesn't, they're still dating the deal from their own calendar.

Metric: Alternative identified. Target: The buyer's answer to "what happens instead" is documented, in the buyer's words, before pricing is discussed. Binary.

Metric: Locating vs. presenting. Target: On the first two calls, the rep's talk time is under 40%. Above that, they're presenting into a position they haven't found yet.

Track named participants across the floor for one quarter. It correlates with
close rate more tightly than activity volume does, and it's a number a manager
can pull without listening to a single call.

Failure mode

Treating the early phase as neutral. The rep believes the negotiation
hasn't started, so they behave as though nothing they do is being scored. They
answer a casual question about pricing casually. They mention a discount another
client received. They agree to a timeline without checking it. They volunteer a
weakness to seem candid, which is a real tool, used deliberately, in Chapter
11, and a liability used accidentally here.

Every one of those is a concession, made in a negotiation the rep didn't know
was running, recorded by someone who did.

What it looks like on tape: the phrase "we're still early" or "no pressure
either way," followed within two minutes by a number, a date, or a comparison
to another client.

The recovery: you cannot retract information, and trying to reads as
instability. What you can do is find out what it cost you.

Quote. I threw a number at you earlier that I hadn't thought carefully about. Where did that land on your end? End quote.

That question does two things. It surfaces whether the concession registered, often it didn't, and you've spent a week worrying about nothing. And when it
did register, you get to hear the position it created, in their words, while
there's still time to work on it.

The second failure mode, more common at senior level: assuming that because
you have been doing this for fifteen years, the negotiation starts when you
decide it does. Experience makes reps faster at the pricing conversation and no
better at knowing what happened before it. The reps who lose the most deals to
invisible internal positions are usually the most confident ones in the room.
