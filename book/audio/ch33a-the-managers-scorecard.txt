The Standard

The scorecard is a twelve-row instrument for auditing a recorded negotiation
against the four stages. It produces a number, and more importantly it produces
a specific sentence a manager can say to a rep about a specific moment on a
specific tape.

It scores the call, never the person. That distinction is the whole instrument.
"You're not confident on price" is an assessment of a human being, it cannot be
disproved, and there is nothing to do with it on Monday. "At 4:12 you named a
number, they went quiet, and eleven seconds later you took two points off it
without being asked" is a description of a recording. It is checkable, it is
fixable, and the rep can hear it without defending themselves.

Every negotiation tool in this manual has a metric because of this chapter. A
method that cannot be audited is a philosophy, and philosophies do not survive
contact with a sales floor under quota. The scorecard is what makes the Protocol
a standard rather than a point of view, and it is the reason a manager who has
never met the author can run it correctly.

When to reach for it

Use the scorecard when:
Reviewing any recorded negotiation, in or out of a rollout. It is the
 standing format, not a program artifact.
A deal closed at a worse number than it should have and nobody can say
 where it went. The scorecard finds the moment.
You are coaching someone who is already good. Strong reps plateau on the
 two or three rows they never score on, and they generally cannot see which.
You need to show a number to someone above you. Twelve rows across a
 team, tracked monthly, is a defensible picture of capability.

Do not use it when:
The rep is in the deal right now and emotional about it. Score after, not
 during. A scorecard delivered mid-deal reads as blame and will cost you the
 tape.
You only have your memory of the call. Scoring from recollection produces
 scores about how the call felt, which is exactly what this instrument exists
 to replace.
It is the rep's first week. Score their calls yourself for context, but do
 not walk a new hire through twelve rows of things they did wrong. Use one row.
As an input to compensation. The moment a scorecard touches pay, tape
 submission becomes adversarial and every number in it stops being true. This
 is not a soft preference. It is the failure mode that ends the practice
 permanently. See below.

The words

The scorecard is scored silently, then delivered. These are the four sentences
that carry it.

THE OPEN, before any row is read

Quote. I scored the call, not you. Twelve rows, most calls land around six. If you come out at six you're where the floor is, not in trouble. End quote.

THE BAD ROW, the format that makes a low score usable

Quote. Row 9. At [timestamp] you [observable behavior]. What were you working with there? End quote.

Timestamp, behavior, question. Never an adjective.

THE GOOD ROW, do not skip these, and be as specific

Quote. Row 4. At 2:40 they said 'that's not really the issue' and you said nothing for six seconds. That's where the call turned. End quote.

THE CLOSE, one row, never twelve

Quote. Next two calls, we're only working row 9. Everything else stays as it is. Bring me the tape Thursday. End quote.

The last one is the discipline that makes the instrument work. Twelve rows of
feedback produces zero behavior change. It produces a rep who feels generally
bad at negotiating. One row produces one changed behavior, which is the entire
achievable outcome of a coaching conversation.

What good sounds like

A rep who argues with a specific row. "I did label there, at 3:15." That is
the instrument working exactly as designed. You are now both looking at the
same tape, discussing an observable thing, and one of you is going to be right.
Go to the tape. If they're right, say so in front of the room next time.

A rep who scores their own call before the review. The point at which the
standard has transferred. When self-scores land within a point or two of yours,
that rep no longer needs you to review their calls, and you should tell them so.

Silence on the good rows. Reps generally do not know which of their instincts
are the strong ones, and will drop a good habit for a worse one if nobody ever
named it.

What is not good: agreement. A rep who accepts all twelve rows without
friction has not engaged with any of them.
