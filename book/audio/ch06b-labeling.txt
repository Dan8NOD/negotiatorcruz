The drill

"Three Emotions, Three Minutes." Fifteen minutes, no preparation, run by
a manager with a laptop.
Play three minutes of a recorded call - the team's own, not a demo.
Everyone stays silent and writes down three emotions they believe the
 counterpart is experiencing.
Next to each, they write the evidence: the exact phrase, pause, or
 shift in pace that produced the read.
Around the table. Anyone who wrote "frustrated" with no evidence line
 doesn't get to count it.
Each person then converts their strongest read into a label and says it
 out loud, in full, to the room. The room's job is to catch anything that
 starts with "I" or ends with a question mark.

Run it weekly. The point is not the recording - it's the evidence column.
Reps who can't cite evidence are pattern-matching on tone, and tone is the
first thing that misleads you on a hard call.

Scored on

A manager can audit all four of these from a tape without being on the call.

Metric: Specificity. Target: Label names a specific state ("cornered," "exposed," "worn down"), not a generic one ("frustrated," "concerned," "upset"). Generic labels score zero.

Metric: Landing rate. Target: Percentage of labels that draw "that's right" or a substantive correction. Below 50%, the rep is guessing rather than reading.

Metric: Position. Target: Label was delivered before the first ask in the call. Binary. Yes or no.

Metric: Clean delivery. Target: No "I," no question mark, no follow-on sentence. Binary.

Track landing rate per rep over four weeks. It is the single most predictive
number in the Read stage, and it moves quickly once evidence-writing becomes
a habit.

Failure mode

Solving before diagnosing. This is the most expensive habit in the stage
and it is almost always well-intentioned. The counterpart describes a
problem, the rep feels the pull to be useful, and out comes a fix - a
discount, an expedited timeline, a concession nobody asked for.

The moment you offer a solution, you have told them you were assembling a
response while they were talking. Everything you concede after that reads as
pressure rather than good faith, because it arrived before you demonstrated
you understood what you were solving.

What it looks like on tape: the rep's first substantive sentence contains
a number, a date, or the word "we can."

The recovery: stop, and label the thing you just did.

Quote. I jumped straight to fixing that, and I don't think I actually understood the problem yet. What's the part of this that's hardest for you? End quote.

This works better than it has any right to. You have modeled the exact
behavior you're asking for - naming an uncomfortable truth out loud instead
of defending it - and you have handed the conversation back.

The second failure mode, less common but harder to recover from: the
performed label. Delivered with warmth turned up, eye contact held a half
second too long, the cadence of someone who read a book. Counterparts detect
it instantly and the correct read is that they are being handled. Labels work
because they are unremarkable. Say them flatly, the way you'd say the weather.
