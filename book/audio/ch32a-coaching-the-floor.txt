The Standard

The rollout in Chapter 31 gets the behaviour started. Coaching is what keeps it
alive after the calendar invite stops appearing, and it is a different skill
from negotiating.

The trap is specific and nearly universal: a manager who is good at
negotiating will coach by demonstrating. They listen to a rep's call, hear
the missed label, and explain what they would have said. The rep agrees, thanks
them, and changes nothing - because they now have a better example and still no
new capability. Watching someone competent do a thing does not transfer the
thing.

The operating standard is that coaching is diagnosis and rehearsal, not
demonstration. Your job is to find the one behaviour that is costing the most,
name it precisely, and make them do it badly in front of you until it's less
bad. Everything else - the war stories, the "what I would have done," the
general encouragement - feels like coaching and produces nothing measurable.

When to reach for it
Weekly, per rep, for twenty minutes. More frequent than most floors
 manage and shorter than most managers expect.
Within a week of any new behaviour being taught. Chapter 31's core
 principle: a behaviour not recorded and scored within a week won't survive the
 month.
Immediately after a loss. Not to review the loss - to find which stage
 the rep skipped.
When a metric moves the wrong way. Chapter 33's scorecard tells you where
 to point.

Do not:
Coach more than one thing. The single most common error. A rep leaving a
 session with four corrections implements zero.
Coach in a group what belongs one-to-one. Public correction produces
 compliance and private resentment.
Coach the outcome. "You lost that deal" isn't coachable. "You offered a
 solution at ninety seconds" is.

The words

The coaching conversation, in order
THEIR READ FIRST - always

Quote. Play me the moment you'd do differently. End quote.

Quote. Where do you think it turned? End quote.

Quote. What stage were you actually in there? End quote.
NAME ONE THING - specific, behavioural, from the tape

Quote. You asked a good calibrated question at four minutes and then answered it yourself at four-oh-three. End quote.

Quote. There was no Disarm in that call at all. End quote.

Quote. Your talk time was sixty-eight percent. End quote.
MAKE THEM DO IT - now, in the room

Quote. Run that thirty seconds again. I'll be them. End quote.

Quote. Say the label out loud. Just the label. End quote.

Quote. Ask it again and hold six seconds. I'll count. End quote.
AGREE THE REP - one behaviour, this week, measurable

Quote. This week: label before the first ask, every call. I'll check three tapes on Friday. End quote.

Step 1 is not politeness. A rep who identifies their own error retains the
correction; a rep who is told the error retains that they were told something.
Ask first, every time, even when you already know the answer - especially
when you already know the answer.

Step 3 is where most coaching sessions end prematurely, because rehearsal is
awkward for both people and the conversation has already felt productive.
Skipping it is what makes coaching a conversation instead of a training event.

When a rep resists

Quote. It sounds like this feels like criticism rather than coaching. End quote.

Quote. It seems like you've had feedback before that didn't go anywhere. End quote.

Quote. What's the part of this you disagree with? End quote.

Quote. Would it be unfair to ask what's actually in the way? End quote.

Chapter 14 applies to you here. A defending rep is not learning, and the fastest
route out is to name the defence rather than push through it.

Confirming a behaviour is real

One agreement is a data point. The same commitment confirmed three separate
ways is a pattern.

Quote. 1. Plain commitment: "So you'll label before the ask. End quote.

Quote. 2. Summarized back: "The change is: no ask until the emotion's been named. That's right? End quote.

Quote. 3. A how question: "How will you remember to do that on the fourth call of the morning? End quote.

If it wobbles on the third pass, the commitment isn't real, and you have found
that out on Monday rather than at the end of the month.

What good sounds like

They name the error before you do. The goal state. It means they're
listening to their own calls, which is the habit that makes coaching
unnecessary.

Visible discomfort during rehearsal. Good sign. Rehearsal that feels easy is
rehearsal of something already learned.

A worse first attempt than their real call. Expected and correct.
Performing a behaviour deliberately is harder than performing it accidentally,
and the dip precedes the improvement.

They ask for a specific tape. "Can we look at the Henderson call, I think I
lost it in the audit." Self-directed coaching - the point of the whole
programme.

What good does not sound like: enthusiastic agreement and warm thanks. The
most pleasant coaching sessions are usually the least effective ones. If nobody
was uncomfortable, nobody rehearsed.
