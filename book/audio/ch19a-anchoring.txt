The Standard

The first number said out loud bends every number that follows it, including
the ones said by people who know it's happening.

That is anchoring, and there are three moves in it. Probe theirs - find out
what a number is actually built on, because most numbers are constructed rather
than calculated. Bracket the range - establish the field before anyone
commits to a point on it. Set yours - deliberately, with a structure, so
your concessions signal a floor instead of an invitation.

Most commercial training covers only the third and does it badly, usually as
"anchor high." Anchoring high into a buyer whose number you haven't tested is
not a strategy, it's a coin flip. The order in this chapter is the order in the
field: probe, bracket, then set.

When to reach for it

Probe when a number lands - theirs, a competitor's, a benchmark, a
"budget." Any figure that arrives as a fact.

Bracket when nobody has committed yet and you want the field defined before
a point is. Ranges are much easier to get than numbers, and a range tells you
almost as much.

Set when you have a read on their range and you're first to a firm figure.

Do not:
Anchor before you've probed. A high anchor into an unknown range either
 lands absurd or leaves money on the table, and you won't know which.
Probe a number you've already accepted. Interrogating a figure you agreed
 to last week reads as bad faith.
Bracket when you already know. Asking for a range you can calculate makes
 you look like you're fishing, because you are.

The words

Probe theirs

The shipped trigger: they threw out a number. Test what it's built on.

Anchor Probe.

Quote. Where did that number actually come from? End quote.

Quote. What's the story behind that specific figure? End quote.

Quote. Compared to what, exactly? End quote.

Quote. That number's doing a lot of work. What's actually behind it? End quote.

Quote. That figure sounds borrowed. Whose is it? End quote.

Quote. Is that the number, or just the first one anyone said out loud? End quote.

Quote. What number were you anchored to before you walked in here? End quote.

Quote. Is that number based on this deal, or the last one that went badly? End quote.

Quote. What's the reference point you're using to call this unfair? End quote.

Quote. This number feels pre-loaded. What are you really measuring against? End quote.

Quote. If you'd never heard that number, what would you have guessed instead? End quote.

Quote. What would have to change for that number to move? End quote.

"Compared to what, exactly?" - three words, and the highest-yield sentence
in the chapter. Every claim that something is expensive is a comparison with
the second half missing. Ask for the second half and roughly half the time the
comparison turns out to be a different product, a different scope, a
three-year-old quote, or a number somebody made up in a meeting.

"Is that the number, or just the first one anyone said out loud?" is the
one to keep for internal-sounding figures. Budgets are frequently the residue
of a single unchallenged sentence in a planning session, and asking this
politely invites your counterpart to admit it - which they often want to,
because they didn't set it either.

"Is that number based on this deal, or the last one that went badly?" Half
of all aggressive anchors are grief about a previous vendor wearing a price
tag. This question surfaces that without accusing anyone of it.

Bracket the range

Bracket.

Quote. Before we talk exact numbers, what range are you even considering? End quote.

Quote. Most deals like this land between two numbers. Where do you see it? End quote.

Quote. Let's bracket it - too low, too high, and somewhere in between. End quote.

Quote. If we're both guessing right now, what's your honest range? End quote.

Quote. If you had to guess a range blind, what would you say? End quote.

Quote. If we could wave a magic wand, what range would feel fair? End quote.

Quote. What range would make this an easy conversation instead of a hard one? End quote.

Quote. Let's set a range first - what's the highest you'd go? End quote.

Quote. What's the low end you'd still feel good about? End quote.

Quote. What would the top of your range need to include to make sense? End quote.

Quote. Where does this stop feeling worth it for you? End quote.

Quote. What's the number where you'd stop and think twice? End quote.

People who will not give you a number will give you a range, and the top of a
volunteered range is almost always above the number they'd have named. That
gap is free.

"What would the top of your range need to include to make sense?" is the
best of the twelve because it does two jobs - it gets the ceiling and it
gets the specification that justifies the ceiling, which is your build list for
the proposal.

Set yours - the Ackerman ladder

When you're setting, use a structure rather than instinct. The ladder from the
1st Edition still holds:

Step: 1. Offer: 65% of your target. What it signals: Opening. Deliberately uncomfortable.

Step: 2. Offer: 85%. What it signals: You move - but by a large amount, once.

Step: 3. Offer: 95%. What it signals: Decelerating. They can feel the floor now.

Step: 4. Offer: 100%. What it signals: Final. Use a precise, non-round number.

Two rules make it work, and they are the parts people drop.

The steps must decelerate. 65 → 85 → 95 → 100 gives concessions of 20, 10,
A counterpart tracking the pattern can extrapolate that the next move is
worth almost nothing, which is the entire signal. Even steps say the well is
infinite.

End on a non-round number. $47,250 reads as calculated. $47,000 reads as
chosen, and anything chosen can be re-chosen. Round numbers invite one more
round.

Between steps: silence (Chapter 8), and a trade (Chapter 18). A ladder run
without trades is just a slow discount.

What good sounds like

The number gets a story, and the story is thin. "Honestly it's what we
budgeted last year plus a bit." You now know the anchor has nothing under it.

A range where the top surprises you. They were prepared to go higher than
you'd have guessed. This happens often enough to justify always bracketing.

"Compared to the other quote" - followed by a scope that isn't yours.
Extremely common and usually resolves the objection entirely.

They defend the number with detail. Real, calculated numbers come with
structure. If the defense is specific and consistent, believe it and go find a
trade instead.

What good does not sound like: irritation at the probe. If "where did that
number come from?" lands badly, the delivery was sharp, not the question. Flat
and curious (Chapter 8).
