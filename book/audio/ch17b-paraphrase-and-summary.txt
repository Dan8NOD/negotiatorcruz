Chapter 17 - Paraphrase and Summary

Part 2 of 2 · The Practice

Part III · STEER

Part 1 of 2 covers the standard, when to reach for it, the words, and what good sounds like.

The drill

"Three, In Their Words." Twenty minutes, pairs, recorded.
The counterpart talks about a real, complicated situation for four
 minutes. The operator may use encouragers and nothing else.
The operator then delivers a summary in three items, and must use the
 counterpart's own vocabulary - their nouns, their adjectives, their framing.
 No translation into the operator's language.
The counterpart scores it out of three. One point per item they'd sign.
Then the operator delivers the "isolate" version: what's agreed, what's
 open.
Swap.

The scoring is what makes it work. Reps consistently believe they summarized
accurately and consistently score two out of three, and the missing item is
almost always the emotional one - they capture the logistics and drop the part
about being burned before.

The variant worth running: ban the operator from using any word the
counterpart didn't use. It's harder than it sounds and it cures the habit of
upgrading someone's plain description into your own commercial vocabulary,
which is how "we got surprised by an invoice" becomes "billing transparency
concerns" and stops being a real thing anyone said.

Scored on

Metric: Summary delivered before Close. Target: Binary, per deal. On most floors this is simply absent.

Metric: Item count. Target: Three. Not two, not five.

Metric: Their vocabulary. Target: Percentage of the summary's key nouns that came from the counterpart. Under 70% means it's been translated into your language.

Metric: Accuracy score. Target: Items the counterpart would sign, out of three. Track over a month.

Metric: Open item isolated. Target: Did the summary end by naming exactly what remains? Binary - this is the one with revenue attached.

Metric: Paraphrase before disagreement. Target: Every disagreement preceded by a paraphrase.

Failure mode

Summarizing your own pitch. The rep announces a summary and then recaps the
value proposition: "So we've talked about the implementation support, the SLA,
and the pricing flexibility…"

None of that is their position. It's yours, restated, wearing the costume of a
summary. The counterpart hears a close being set up and re-braces, and the
tool - which they would have found genuinely useful - is spent.

What it looks like on tape: the summary's three items are all things the
rep introduced.

The recovery: name the switch and hand it over.

Quote. That was my summary, not yours - let me try again. Everything you've told me: correct anything I get wrong. End quote.

The second failure mode: the summary that omits the inconvenient item. The
rep summarizes three things and quietly drops the objection they don't have an
answer for. Counterparts notice this every single time, and what they conclude
is not that you forgot. A summary's authority comes entirely from its
completeness - the moment it becomes selective it converts from an instrument of
trust into evidence of manoeuvring, and it takes the rest of the meeting with
it.
