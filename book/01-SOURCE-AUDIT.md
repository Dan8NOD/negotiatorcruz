# Source Audit — *NOD Academy, 1st Edition*

**Read in full:** 34 sections, 33 chapters, ~18,100 words.
**Subtitle:** *The Practice-First Negotiation Manual for the Conversations That Define Your Life*

This is the diagnostic pass. What the book currently is, what is suppressing
the consulting rate, and what survives the rewrite intact.

---

## 1. What the 1st Edition actually is

It's a **consumer self-improvement manual with a training community attached**,
and it's a good one for that job. The chapter template is already
manual-shaped (Core idea → body → Real-world example → The move → Try it this
week → Nugget → ASCII diagram). The instincts are right. The *category* is wrong
for a $15–20K corporate install.

Current structure: three Levels — Listening, Dialogue, Collaboration — with the
Staircase metaphor and a graduation test at each level (free coffee, free
upgrade, angry neighbor in 60 seconds).

---

## 2. The five things suppressing the rate

Ranked by how much each one costs.

### 2.1 The book is addressed to individuals, not organizations

Seven of 33 chapters — 21% of the book — are consumer scenarios:

> Ch 17 Salary Negotiation · Ch 18 Buying a Car · Ch 19 Renting an Apartment ·
> Ch 20 Real Estate Deals · Ch 21 Contractor / Service Quotes ·
> Ch 22 Difficult Conversations with Family · Ch 23 Conflict at Work

Plus the Level tests: negotiate a free coffee, negotiate a free hotel upgrade.

This is the single most expensive problem in the book. A VP of Sales opens the
Amazon sample, sees "negotiate a free cup of coffee" in the first paragraph of
the introduction, and files the author under consumer self-help — a category
with a hard ceiling around $5K and no committee approval path. Everything else
in the rewrite is downstream of fixing this.

Note that the business has **already made this split**: negotiatorcruz.com is
the corporate brand, negotiatorsondemand.com is where consumer products live,
and the corporate site links to it as a separate destination. The book has not
caught up to the business.

### 2.2 The dream material is load-bearing and stated outright

Not subtext — architecture. From the front matter:

> "The 33 chapters in this book all trace back to five dreams. Not five ideas.
> Five actual dreams I had, recorded, and turned into lessons."

The introduction pre-announces it ("The dreams are real"), a full front-matter
section maps each dream to the chapters it anchors, and a back-matter section
titled **The Unseen Layer** closes the loop:

> "Each chapter in this manual is anchored by a dream-sourced principle. The
> reader gets the tactic. You know the root… The reader feels the weight but
> never sees the dream. That's the point."

Two separate problems there. The dream sourcing itself is disqualifying for a
procurement reviewer. But *The Unseen Layer* is worse — it reads as internal
author notes shipped by accident, and what it tells a paying reader is that the
book has a hidden layer being withheld from them. Nothing costs authority faster
with a corporate buyer than the suggestion that the real system is elsewhere.

**The good news, and it's genuinely good:** the five dream principles are sound
negotiation doctrine on their own merits. They don't need the dreams.

| Dream | The principle underneath | Survives as |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · White Shirt Protocol | Presence is established before you speak | The Read stage opener — composure as a diagnostic instrument |
| 2 · Office Right Turn | Walkaway power; don't chase, don't justify | BATNA chapter — already the strongest chapter in the book |
| 3 · Flattening to 2D | Strip the drama, isolate the actual problem | The de-escalation discipline running through Disarm |
| 4 · Power Strip Past the Receptionist | Talk to whoever can actually say yes | Reading the decision structure — a genuinely differentiated chapter |
| 5 · Chutzpah Throne Strike | One precise move, then stillness | The Close — restraint as technique |

Every one of those is defensible on mechanism alone. **We keep the doctrine and
drop the sourcing.** Nothing of substance is lost.

### 2.3 The book and the website teach two different systems

The book teaches **three Levels** (Listening → Dialogue → Collaboration).
The website sells **four Stages** (Read → Disarm → Steer → Close), branded as
the Cruz Protocol.

A buyer who reads the book and then visits the site encounters two
architectures with different names, different counts, and no stated
relationship. That fragments authority at exactly the moment it should be
compounding — and it makes the method look like it's still being figured out.

**Recommended reconciliation** (they're measuring different axes, so both can
live):

- **Stages = what you run inside a negotiation.** Read → Disarm → Steer →
  Close. Sequential, every time. This becomes the book's spine.
- **Levels = the order in which an operator is certified.** Level 1 → 2 → 3.
  A progression through competence, not through a conversation.

That's not a compromise — it's what the two things actually are, and separating
them cleanly gives you the thing the ladder is currently missing: a
**certification tier**, where a client's own managers are licensed to run the
Protocol internally and you're paid annually instead of per-event.

### 2.4 The book is too short to carry the price

18,100 words across 33 chapters averages ~450 words per chapter. On Kindle at
$10 that's fine. In print at $50, and as the credentialing artifact behind a
$20K engagement, it is thin — and a corporate buyer will register that
physically before they register it intellectually.

Target ~70,000 words. Roughly 2,000 per chapter. That is not padding: the
seven-part chapter spine (standard, triggers, scripts, what good sounds like,
drill, scoring, failure mode) fills that space with operational content that
isn't currently in the book at all.

### 2.5 The back matter contradicts the front matter

Page 2 establishes **The NOD Pledge**:

> "I will use these tools to create clarity, not confusion. More dignity, not
> less… the other person will not feel beaten. They will feel heard."

The back matter, in *From the Masters*, then offers:

> "Justify their failures — 'It's not your fault, the system is rigged' —
> instant loyalty"
> "Help them throw rocks at their enemies — give them a shared target"
> "Confirm their suspicions"
> "**Forbidden Tactics**" · "**Surveys are covert persuasion**"

An enterprise ethics or compliance reviewer will find this in about four
minutes, and the contradiction with the Pledge is the part that does the
damage — not the tactics themselves. The Pledge is a genuine asset and a real
differentiator; keep it, and cut anything that undercuts it.

Also flagged in the same section: the Feedback Framework ground rules include
**"Shamelessly promote NOD services."** That's an instructor note that shipped
to readers. Cut.

**Attribution.** Voss is currently framed as the origin of the method in the
introduction, then compressed to six bullets in the back matter. The website
handles this considerably better — tools are FBI-derived and cited as such;
the proprietary contribution is the sequencing, the drills, and the scoring.
Move the book to the site's position. It's both more accurate and more
defensible, and "I didn't invent the tools, I built the system for installing
them" is a stronger claim to a corporate buyer than an origin story.

**One factual flag.** Ch 7 states: "Functional MRI studies show that naming a
negative emotion reduces amygdala activation… You just changed their brain
chemistry with six words." The underlying research is real (affect-labeling
work out of UCLA), but the claim as written overreaches and the citation is
missing. Corporate audiences contain people who check. Cite it properly or
soften it — `[NEEDS: decision]`.

## 3. What survives intact

Not a teardown. Real assets in here:

- **The chapter template.** Core idea / The move / Try it this week / Nugget is
  already operational thinking. It gets extended, not replaced.
- **The ASCII diagrams.** Genuinely good — the Label Decision Tree and the
  Salary Playbook are the best pages in the book. These convert directly into
  the Field Cards, which become the training-day leave-behind.
- **The NOD Pledge.** Keep it, move it forward, make it the ethical standard the
  method is sold on. Corporate buyers care about this more than consumers do —
  it's what lets a sales director defend the training internally.
- **The practice-first conviction.** "You cannot learn this by reading" is the
  right position for a book to take, and it's what makes the book a funnel
  rather than a substitute for the engagement.
- **Ch 31's logging discipline.** Three sentences after every rep — what I did,
  what they did, what I'd do differently. That's already 80% of a manager's
  coaching instrument. It becomes Ch 33.
- **The credentials.** Five years, 1,000+ sessions, weekly live coaching,
  Marine, Chicago. Specific, verifiable-sounding, and currently buried.
- **Ch 2 (Walkaway Power).** The strongest chapter in the book as written.
  Barely needs work beyond the corporate reframe.

## 5. Open decisions

Two, and both change the shape of 33 chapters. Flagged for Dan:

1. **Levels vs. Stages** — adopt the reconciliation in §2.3 (Stages = the
   sequence you run, Levels = the certification ladder), or keep the book on
   Levels alone and revise the website instead?
2. **Replace or fork** — does this become the 2nd edition, superseding the
   consumer version, or a separate corporate edition running alongside it under
   the negotiatorcruz.com brand?
