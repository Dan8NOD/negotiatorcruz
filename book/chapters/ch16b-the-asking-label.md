# Chapter 16 — The Asking Label

### Part 2 of 2 · The Practice

**Part III · STEER**

*Part 1 of 2 covers the standard, when to reach for it, the words, and what good sounds like.*

---

## The drill

**"Kill the Question."** Fifteen minutes, no preparation.

1. Each person lists the **five questions they most want answered** across
   their live pipeline. The real ones — budget, competition, authority,
   deadline, the actual obstacle.
2. Next to each, write **what asking it directly would reveal about you.**
   This column is the uncomfortable one and it's the point of the drill.
3. Convert each into an asking label. No question marks allowed.
4. Say all five out loud, then hold six seconds after each — the silence is
   half the tool.
5. Pick the deal where you've been avoiding a direct question for weeks and
   commit to the label version this week.

The reliable discovery is in column two. Most reps have never considered that
their questions are also disclosures, and seeing five of their own laid out
that way changes how they open calls permanently.

---

## Scored on

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| **Disclosure avoided** | Sensitive intelligence — budget, competition, authority, deadline — obtained without a direct question. Count per deal. |
| **Correction rate** | Percentage of asking labels that draw a correction or an extension rather than a bare yes. Above 60% means the reads are calibrated. |
| **Silence held** | Six seconds after each. Binary. |
| **Density** | One per exchange. Two in a row scores as a failure. |
| **Accuracy band** | Was the read roughly 70% right? Too safe produces bare agreement; too wrong is a mislabel and belongs in Ch 9. |

---

## Failure mode

**Turning it back into a question.** The rep delivers the label correctly and
then, in the silence, panics and appends the question they were avoiding:
*"...it seems like you're comparing this against something else — are you
talking to anyone else?"*

Everything is undone in six words. The disclosure they were protecting is now
made, the label reads as a manipulation that preceded the real question, and
the counterpart has learned to distrust the next label.

**What it looks like on tape:** an asking label followed within four seconds by
a sentence ending in a question mark.

**The recovery:** none available mid-call, which is why this one matters. Once
the question is asked it is asked. The only fix is upstream — drill the six
seconds until the reflex holds.

**The second failure mode: stacking them.** Three asking labels in ninety
seconds stops being perceptive and starts feeling like being read. The
counterpart goes quiet, gives shorter answers, and the rest of the meeting
yields nothing. One asking label per exchange, and never immediately after a
label from another family — the counterpart cannot distinguish between your
families, they only register that they are being narrated at.
