# Chapter 24 — Reframing

### Part 1 of 2 · The Method

**Part IV · CLOSE**

---

## The Standard

A reframe changes what the negotiation is about without changing what's on the
table.

Two moves do it. **Vision labels** pull someone out of the problem and into the
outcome — the shipped trigger is exact: *they're stuck in the problem. Pull
them to the outcome.* **Elevation labels** raise the other side's standing, so
that agreeing becomes consistent with who they are rather than a concession
they made.

Both work on the same principle and it is worth stating plainly, because it is
the difference between a reframe and a manipulation: **you are not changing the
facts, you are changing which facts are in the foreground.** A deal that looks
like a cost when viewed as a line item looks like a decision when viewed from
twelve months out. Both views are true. Which one is in front of them is
negotiable, and largely up to you.

---

## When to reach for it

**Vision labels** when:

- **They're circling the problem.** Third meeting, same objection, no movement.
  The frame is stuck, not the terms.
- **The negotiation has become arithmetic.** Once it's purely a gap between two
  numbers, whoever has the tighter constraint wins. Reframing is how you leave
  that terrain.
- **The real value doesn't show up on the invoice.** Which is most of the time.

**Elevation labels** when:

- **They're behaving smaller than they are.** Deferring, hedging, hiding behind
  process.
- **You need them to act with authority they do have.** An elevation label is
  frequently the fastest route to someone using power they've been declining to
  use.
- **Before a hard number.** *"You seem like someone who doesn't get rattled by
  a hard number"* costs nothing and changes how the next sentence lands.

Do **not** reach for either when:

- **The problem is real and unaddressed.** Reframing an unsolved problem is
  spin, and it is recognized as spin instantly. Solve it or name it first.
- **The elevation isn't true.** Same rule as Chapter 13's positive labels.
  Untrue elevation is flattery, it's detectable, and it costs more than it buys.
- **You've used one in the last few minutes.** These are strong. One at a time.

## The words

### Vision labels — pull them to the outcome

```
VISION — the future state

  "What does this look like once it's actually working?"
  "What does the day after this deal closes actually look like for you?"
  "What needs to be true a year from now for this to feel like a win?"
  "If this goes exactly right, what's different about how you operate
   six months from now?"
  "What does 'better' actually look like once this is behind you?"
  "If this works perfectly, what changes for you beyond the money?"

VISION — name what they're really doing

  "It sounds like you're not just negotiating the deal — you're
   negotiating the future."
  "It sounds like you're protecting a future version of this,
   not just today."
  "It seems like you're solving for a future problem, not just today's."
  "It sounds like you're already picturing the version of this
   that works."

VISION — the unspoken want

  "What's the version of this that you're afraid to say out loud?"
  "It sounds like the outcome you really want isn't on the table yet."
  "What would you regret not asking for once this is done?"
```

**"What would you regret not asking for once this is done?"** is the most
valuable line in the family and it belongs at the close. It gives them
permission to raise the thing they've been holding — and the thing people hold
back is very often cheap for you and decisive for them. This is Chapter 25's
territory arriving early, and it is worth asking twice in a long deal.

**"What's the version of this that you're afraid to say out loud?"** is the
sharpest and requires the most earned trust. Do not use it before Stage 2 is
done. Used at the right moment it produces the actual objective, which is
frequently not the one that's been on the agenda for three meetings.

### Elevation labels — raise their standing

```
ELEVATION

  "It seems like you've done this a hundred times before."
  "It sounds like you know this market better than anyone
   I've spoken to."
  "It looks like you were three moves ahead before we started."
  "It looks like you already knew where this was headed
   before I said it."
  "You seem like someone who doesn't need this explained twice."
  "It seems like you've watched every version of this play out already."
  "It sounds like your instincts on this are better than most
   people's research."
  "It sounds like people come to you when they actually want
   it done right."
  "You seem like the person in the room everyone else defers to."
  "It seems like you've built something most people only talk
   about building."
  "It looks like you've earned the right to be particular about
   who you work with."
  "You seem like someone who doesn't get rattled by a hard number."
```

The mechanism is consistency, not flattery. A person who has just been
described — accurately — as someone who's done this a hundred times will not
then behave like a novice, because people act in line with the identity that's
currently active. That is why the truth requirement is absolute: an elevation
label that isn't true doesn't activate an identity, it activates suspicion.

Two of these are structurally different and worth noticing.

**"It looks like you've earned the right to be particular about who you work
with."** reframes their scrutiny as earned standing rather than obstruction.
Extremely useful with a difficult buyer, because it removes the fight over
whether their difficulty is legitimate.

**"You seem like someone who doesn't get rattled by a hard number."** is
delivered *before* the number. It costs one sentence and it changes what
flinching would mean.

---

## What good sounds like

**They describe the future in detail.** The longer the description, the more
committed they are to it — and everything they name becomes something the deal
now has to deliver, which is your specification.

**"Honestly, what I really want is…"** The vision label surfacing the actual
objective. Usually different from the stated one and usually more achievable.

**They act bigger.** Observable within a minute of an elevation label. Shorter
sentences, fewer qualifiers, decisions made rather than deferred.

**They correct the elevation downward — and then act on it anyway.** *"I
wouldn't say everyone defers to me…"* followed by a decision. The identity
activated regardless of whether they accepted the compliment.

**What good does not sound like:** a joke deflecting the elevation, followed by
nothing changing. That's a label that read as flattery. Stop using them with
this person and go back to substance.
