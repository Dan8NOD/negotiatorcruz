# Chapter 10 — The Accusation Audit

**Part II · DISARM**

---

## The Standard

Before you ask for anything, you say out loud the worst things the other side
is privately thinking about you.

Not softened. Not reframed into a strength. Said plainly, in your own voice,
while they still believe you don't know they're thinking it.

That is the accusation audit, and it is the single most counterintuitive
instruction in this manual. Every instinct a commercial professional has says
to lead with credibility and let the negatives surface later, if at all. That
instinct is wrong, and it is wrong for a mechanical reason: **an objection you
have already said out loud cannot be used against you.** It has been spent.
Whatever ammunition it carried, you fired it yourself, into the air, at a
moment of your choosing.

The alternative is that they hold it — through your presentation, through your
pricing, through your close — and deploy it at the point of maximum damage,
which is invariably the moment you have run out of things to say.

---

## When to reach for it

Reach for it:

- **Before the ask. Always.** The audit is the last thing you do in Stage 2 and
  the ask is the first thing you do in Stage 3. That's the order.
- **When you can feel resistance building and nothing has been said yet.** This
  is the shipped trigger for the whole family: *you sense resistance building
  before you've asked.* That feeling is usually accurate and it is always
  actionable.
- **When you genuinely are the weaker party.** New vendor, higher price, worse
  reputation, smaller company. The audit is at its strongest exactly where you
  are.
- **When you're walking into a room that has already decided about you.**
  Chapter 1's territory. If the negotiation started without you, the objections
  did too.

Do **not** reach for it:

- **When nothing is loaded.** An audit delivered into a genuinely warm room
  invents a problem and then hands it to them. If you can't name a specific
  thing they're likely thinking, you don't have an audit — you have anxiety.
- **After they've already said the objection.** Once it's out loud it's theirs
  and you handle it as a live objection, not as an audit. Auditing something
  they just said reads as mockery.
- **In a compressed exchange.** A two-minute call has no room for it. Save it
  for the conversation where the ask actually lands.

---

## The words

The construction is fixed and it does the work: **"you probably think," "it
might seem like," "it probably looks like."** You are not agreeing that the
accusation is true. You are stating, accurately, that it is available to them.

```
ACCUSATION AUDIT — your motives

  "You probably think I'm just trying to squeeze you here."
  "It probably looks like I'm only here for what I can get out of this."
  "It probably looks like I'm more interested in closing this than
   getting it right."
  "You probably think the friendly tone is just a setup for the ask."
  "It might seem like I don't care how this affects you."

ACCUSATION AUDIT — your credibility

  "You might be thinking I don't actually know what I'm talking about."
  "You might think I'm reading off a script right now."
  "It probably feels like this conversation was scripted before I even
   sat down."
  "It probably seems like you've heard this exact pitch before."
  "You probably think the last person who said this to you was lying too."

ACCUSATION AUDIT — what happens after signing

  "You might be thinking I'll disappear the second this is signed."
  "You might think this is going to cost more than I'm letting on."
  "It might look like I'm rushing you into something you're not
   ready for."
  "It might seem like I'm overstepping something that isn't mine
   to touch."
  "It might look like I'm hiding something — I'm not. Ask me anything."
```

Three rules, and the second one is where almost everyone fails.

**Lead with the two that hurt most.** Rank your list by how much damage the
accusation would do if they said it at the worst moment, and open with the top
two. Auditing the mild ones is theatre — they can hear that you've picked the
safe items, and picking the safe items is itself a tell.

**Say it and stop.** Do not rebut. Do not explain. Do not append "…but I want
you to know that's not the case." The instant you defend, the audit converts
back into an argument, and now you're arguing a point they never had to raise.
Chapter 14 is entirely about this discipline because it is the failure mode of
the whole stage.

**Deliver it flat.** Same tone as the easy part of the conversation (Chapter
8). An audit delivered with a nervous laugh is a confession. Delivered evenly,
it's an observation about the world.

Note the one exception in the catalog — *"It might look like I'm hiding
something — I'm not. Ask me anything."* — which does contain a rebuttal. It
works because the rebuttal is an invitation rather than a defense, and because
it hands them the next move. Use it sparingly; the other fourteen follow the
no-defense rule.

---

## What good sounds like

**They laugh, and something loosens.** The most common good outcome. Naming the
unsaid thing releases pressure that had nowhere to go, and the laugh is that
release. The room is measurably different for the next ten minutes.

**"Well — yeah, honestly, that is what I was thinking."** They confirm it. You
have just been handed permission to address the real objection instead of the
polite one, and you have their agreement that it was live.

**They upgrade it.** *"It's not that I think you'll disappear — it's that the
last vendor's implementation team was three contractors we'd never met."* Far
better than being right. You aimed at a category and they handed you the
specific.

**They defend you.** *"No, no, that's not fair — you've been straight with us."*
The tool working at its strangest. Volunteering the negative frequently
produces someone arguing on your behalf, which is a position they now have to
stay consistent with.

**The temperature drops.** Chapter 2's gate for leaving Disarm. If it rises
instead, the audit was aimed at the wrong objection — go back to Read.

**What good does not sound like:** silence and a blank face. That's an audit
that named something they weren't thinking. Move on quickly and don't repeat
it.

---

## The drill

**"The Five Worst Things."** Twenty minutes, run once per quarter and again
before any major deal.

1. Each person writes **the five worst things** the other side privately
   believes about their offer, their company, or them personally. Written, not
   discussed — the writing is where the honesty happens.
2. Rank all five by **how much it would hurt if said at the worst possible
   moment.** Not by likelihood. By damage.
3. Convert the top two into audit lines, verbatim, on the page.
4. Deliver both out loud to the room. The room's only job is to catch a
   defense — any "but," any "the reality is," any explanation.
5. Then the hard round: **deliver them again to someone whose face stays
   completely neutral.** Most people can audit to a friendly audience. The
   field does not supply one.

The reliable discovery in step 2 is that reps have been ranking by likelihood
and therefore auditing the wrong things. The objection most likely to be raised
is rarely the one that would do the most damage.

## Scored on

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| **Audit present at all** | Binary, before the first ask. On most untrained floors this is simply absent — measure it in week one. |
| **Severity selection** | Did they audit the top-two damaging items, or the safe ones? Scored against their own written list. |
| **No defense** | Zero rebuttal sentences following the audit. Binary. This is the one that fails most. |
| **Temperature change** | Did the room cool or heat? Scored by a listener from tape. |
| **Position** | Delivered before the ask, not after an objection. Binary. |

The "no defense" row is the whole chapter. A floor that audits and then defends
has spent the cost of the tool and received none of the benefit — they've
raised objections *for* the buyer and then argued with them.

---

## Failure mode

**Auditing the safe ones.** The rep names something mildly unflattering —
*"you probably think we're a bit smaller than the incumbent"* — feels brave,
and leaves the actual objection sitting untouched. The buyer registers that the
rep picked the comfortable item, which is worse than not auditing at all,
because it demonstrates that the rep knows how the tool works and lacks the
nerve to use it.

**What it looks like on tape:** the audit is about company size, or newness, or
some abstraction. Real audits are about motive, competence, and abandonment.

**The recovery:** go again, properly, and name what you just did.

```
  "That wasn't the real one. The real one is that you think
   the number is high and you've been burned by a vendor
   who promised the same things we're promising."
```

Naming your own hedge is itself an audit, and it lands harder than the audit
you should have opened with.

**The second failure mode: the audit that's actually a humblebrag.** *"You
probably think we're too thorough."* *"You might think we care too much about
getting this right."* This is not an audit. It is a compliment wearing a
disguise, everyone in the room can see it, and it costs more credibility than
saying nothing would have. If the accusation you're voicing wouldn't sting to
hear said about you sincerely, it isn't one.
