# Chapter 27 — Procurement and the Committee You Can't See

## The Standard

Procurement's job is to make you interchangeable.

That is not hostility, it is the function. A procurement professional is
measured on savings and on risk reduction, and the fastest route to both is to
establish that three vendors are equivalent and then run a price comparison.
Everything they do — the RFP template, the scoring matrix, the insistence on
written responses, the refusal to let you speak to the business — serves that
single objective.

Which produces the operating standard: **your job is not to beat procurement.
It is to be un-comparable before procurement is involved, and to survive
comparison when it is.**

And underneath that sits the harder problem. The decision happens in a room you
are not in, described by someone who is not you, to people who have never met
you. **Your argument has to survive being repeated by a third party.** That
single constraint should govern how you build every proposal, and almost nobody
designs for it.

---

## When to reach for it

- **The moment a procurement contact appears.** The window for changing the
  frame closes fast.
- **When you're asked for written responses only.** That's the commoditization
  step. It's not personal and it is not neutral.
- **When your champion goes quiet mid-process.** Usually means the process has
  moved somewhere they don't control.

Do **not**:

- **Try to go around procurement.** It occasionally works and it permanently
  costs you the relationship with the person who administers your contract for
  the next three years.
- **Treat the scoring matrix as the real decision.** It is the justification
  for a decision, which is a different artifact.
- **Assume silence is neutral.** Chapter 20 — test the clock, and test the
  quiet.

## The words

### Build for the room you're not in

Ask the questions whose answers make your argument portable.

```
DESIGNING FOR THE ABSENT ROOM

  "When this gets discussed without me in the room, who
   presents it?"
  "What does that person need from me to make the case well?"
  "What's the question you think will come up that I
   haven't answered?"
  "If someone in that meeting wanted to argue against
   this, what's the strongest thing they'd say?"
  "What would you need in writing to defend this
   internally?"
```

*"If someone in that meeting wanted to argue against this, what's the strongest
thing they'd say?"* is the most valuable question in this chapter. It surfaces
the objection that will be raised in your absence, by the person whose
reputation depends on having opposed you — and it lets you answer it in a
document rather than not at all.

### With procurement directly

Procurement responds well to clarity and badly to charm. Chapter 9's tools work
here, delivered flatly.

```
PROCUREMENT

  "It seems like your job here is to make sure we're
   comparable to two other options."
  "It sounds like the decision itself isn't yours, but the
   process is."
  "What's the thing that would get us disqualified?"
  "What does a compliant response look like — I'd rather
   get the form right than be clever."
  "Who set the requirements, and are they in the room
   for the decision?"
```

*"It seems like your job here is to make sure we're comparable to two other
options"* is a power label (Chapter 9) aimed at a function rather than a
person. It's disarming precisely because it's accurate and unresentful, and it
frequently produces a straight answer about how the process actually works.

*"What's the thing that would get us disqualified?"* asks about the floor
rather than the ceiling. Procurement people answer floor questions readily —
it's compliance information, it's their job to provide it, and it is the single
highest-value thing you can know.

### When it goes quiet — the email exit ramp

Written processes stall, and chasing harder makes it worse. A stalled thread
needs an exit ramp, not a follow-up.

```
  "Have you given up on this?"
  "Should I close your file?"
  "Is waiting the better option here?"
```

A short, low-stakes question that sounds like you're **releasing** them gets
replies that "following up on my last email" does not — because it removes an
obligation instead of adding to one. Chapter 12's principle, applied to a
medium where silence is the default failure.

## What good sounds like

**A name for the presenter.** *"It'll be Rachel taking it to the committee."*
Now you know who you're actually writing for.

**The disqualifier, stated.** *"Honestly, if the security questionnaire comes
back with more than two gaps you're out."* Compliance information, freely
given, and it reorders your entire priority list.

**"What would you need in writing?" — answered specifically.** Every item they
name is something you were going to have to supply anyway, now with the
authority of having been requested.

**Procurement becomes an ally on process.** Not on price — they will never help
you on price. But a procurement lead who tells you how to be compliant is
saving you from disqualification, which is worth more.

**What good does not sound like:** warm chat with procurement and no process
detail. Rapport with procurement that produces no compliance information is
just being liked by someone whose job is to be indifferent to that.
