# Chapter 17 — Paraphrase and Summary

**Part III · STEER**

---

## The Standard

A paraphrase says one thing back in your own words. A summary says everything
back, in order, including the parts that disagree.

Both exist for the same reason and it isn't politeness. **Proof of
understanding is the price of admission to Stage 4.** You cannot close a person
who does not believe you understood what they were closing. And understanding
is not demonstrated by claiming it — "I hear you," "totally understand," "makes
sense" — which is why those phrases are worth nothing. It's demonstrated by
reproduction.

The distinction between the two tools is scale and function. A paraphrase is a
checkpoint: one point, mid-conversation, to confirm you're tracking. A summary
is a structural move: it collects the whole negotiation, states where it stands,
and — critically — **isolates what's still open so you can work on it.** Most
reps use the first occasionally and the second never, which is why most
negotiations end without anyone being sure what was agreed.

---

## When to reach for it

**Paraphrase** when:

- **They've said something complicated.** Reproduce it before you build on it.
- **You're about to disagree.** Paraphrase first, then disagree. The
  disagreement lands as a difference of view rather than as evidence you
  weren't listening.
- **They've corrected you.** Say the correction back. This is the single
  cheapest trust-building move available and almost nobody does it.

**Summary** when:

- **You're leaving Stage 3.** The summary is the bridge to Close. Chapter 22 is
  the version that goes hunting for "that's right"; this is the working one.
- **The conversation has sprawled.** Three meetings, four stakeholders, and
  nobody can state where things stand. Whoever summarizes accurately controls
  what the deal is now about.
- **You need to isolate the last open item.** *"Two things agreed, one thing
  still open. Let's look at that one."* That sentence structure closes deals.
- **Before any handoff.** Different room, new stakeholder, next meeting.

Do **not** reach for either when:

- **You'd be summarizing your own position.** A summary that recaps your pitch
  is a pitch. It only works when it's built from their words.
- **Nothing has been established.** Summarizing an empty conversation
  advertises that it was empty.

## The words

### Paraphrase — the checkpoint

```
PARAPHRASE

  "Let me say that back, and you tell me where I'm wrong."
  "So what I'm hearing is, the timing matters more than the number."
  "In your words then: this only works if the risk moves off you."
  "Putting that in my own words — you're fine with the plan,
   just not the pace."
  "Let me reflect that back: you need certainty more than you
   need speed."
  "If I'm tracking right, this comes down to trust, not terms."
  "So the short version is, you're willing but not on this timeline."
  "Restating it plainly: the concept's fine, the exposure isn't."
  "What I'm taking from that is, it's not the idea you're against."
  "Sounds like the real issue was never the price, it was being
   surprised by it."
  "Boiling it down: you'd need a guarantee, not just a promise."
  "Playing it back simply — you're closer to yes than this
   conversation sounds."
```

Notice the shape of most of these: **X, not Y.** *Timing, not the number.
Trust, not terms. The concept's fine, the exposure isn't.* That construction is
doing real work — it demonstrates you've separated the surface objection from
the actual one, which is the thing they most want to know you've done.

*"Let me say that back, and you tell me where I'm wrong"* is the safest opener
in the family because it pre-authorizes the correction. You're not claiming
accuracy; you're requesting an audit. People correct an invited paraphrase
generously and an uninvited one defensively.

*"Playing it back simply — you're closer to yes than this conversation
sounds"* is the outlier and the most aggressive line in the set. It's a
paraphrase that moves them. Use it when the conversation's tone has drifted
more negative than its substance, which happens often and usually goes
unremarked.

### Summary — the structural move

```
SUMMARY — collect everything

  "Here's everything you've told me — correct anything I get wrong."
  "Let me run the whole thing back to you before we go any further."
  "Let me lay out the whole picture so nothing gets lost before
   we move on."
  "Let me pull it together: the money, the timing, and the trust."

SUMMARY — name the three

  "So: you want speed, you need cover, and you've been burned before."
  "So far: you're open to it, you're worried about timing, and you
   want it in writing."
  "Everything on the table right now: the price, the timeline,
   and who signs first."

SUMMARY — isolate what's open

  "Here's where we are: two things agreed, one thing still open.
   Let's look at that one."
  "Quick recap before we go further: two yeses, one open question."
  "Here's the shape of where we landed: aligned on most of it,
   stuck on one point."

SUMMARY — the reframe

  "Pulling the whole thread together — this was never really
   about the number."
  "Tying it together — you're ready, you just need it in writing."
```

**Use three items.** Almost every shipped summary line lands on three, and
that's not decoration. Three is the largest number a person can hold, verify,
and agree to in one pass. Two feels dismissive of a complex position; four
invites them to start negotiating the list instead of the deal.

**The "isolate what's open" group is the commercial one.** *"Two things agreed,
one thing still open"* does something no other sentence in this manual does: it
converts a sprawling negotiation into a single problem, and it does so while
banking the agreements. Nobody re-opens the two things once they've been
counted out loud.

---

## What good sounds like

**"That's right."** The gold standard and the subject of Chapter 22. Here it
tells you the summary was accurate and you're cleared for Stage 4.

**A correction to one item out of three.** Excellent. The other two are now
confirmed, and you've spent one sentence to lock them.

**They add a fourth.** *"...and honestly there's also the question of who owns
support."* A summary reliably surfaces the thing that was never said, because
seeing the list makes the omission visible to them.

**Relief.** Physical, usually. Chapter 5's somatic reads. A person who has been
explaining a complicated position for forty minutes and finally hears it
reproduced correctly exhales.

**What good does not sound like:** "yeah, sounds about right." Approximate
agreement to a summary is a warning. Something in your three is wrong and they
can't be bothered to fix it — which means it will resurface later, at a worse
moment. Push once: *"which part is only about right?"*

---

## The drill

**"Three, In Their Words."** Twenty minutes, pairs, recorded.

1. The counterpart talks about a real, complicated situation for **four
   minutes.** The operator may use encouragers and nothing else.
2. The operator then delivers a summary in **three items**, and must use the
   counterpart's own vocabulary — their nouns, their adjectives, their framing.
   No translation into the operator's language.
3. The counterpart scores it out of three. One point per item they'd sign.
4. Then the operator delivers the "isolate" version: what's agreed, what's
   open.
5. Swap.

The scoring is what makes it work. Reps consistently believe they summarized
accurately and consistently score two out of three, and the missing item is
almost always the emotional one — they capture the logistics and drop the part
about being burned before.

**The variant worth running:** ban the operator from using any word the
counterpart didn't use. It's harder than it sounds and it cures the habit of
upgrading someone's plain description into your own commercial vocabulary,
which is how "we got surprised by an invoice" becomes "billing transparency
concerns" and stops being a real thing anyone said.

---

## Scored on

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| **Summary delivered before Close** | Binary, per deal. On most floors this is simply absent. |
| **Item count** | Three. Not two, not five. |
| **Their vocabulary** | Percentage of the summary's key nouns that came from the counterpart. Under 70% means it's been translated into your language. |
| **Accuracy score** | Items the counterpart would sign, out of three. Track over a month. |
| **Open item isolated** | Did the summary end by naming exactly what remains? Binary — this is the one with revenue attached. |
| **Paraphrase before disagreement** | Every disagreement preceded by a paraphrase. |

## Failure mode

**Summarizing your own pitch.** The rep announces a summary and then recaps the
value proposition: *"So we've talked about the implementation support, the SLA,
and the pricing flexibility…"*

None of that is their position. It's yours, restated, wearing the costume of a
summary. The counterpart hears a close being set up and re-braces, and the
tool — which they would have found genuinely useful — is spent.

**What it looks like on tape:** the summary's three items are all things the
rep introduced.

**The recovery:** name the switch and hand it over.

```
  "That was my summary, not yours — let me try again.
   Everything you've told me: correct anything I get wrong."
```

**The second failure mode: the summary that omits the inconvenient item.** The
rep summarizes three things and quietly drops the objection they don't have an
answer for. Counterparts notice this every single time, and what they conclude
is not that you forgot. A summary's authority comes entirely from its
completeness — the moment it becomes selective it converts from an instrument of
trust into evidence of manoeuvring, and it takes the rest of the meeting with
it.
