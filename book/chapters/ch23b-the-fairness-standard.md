# Chapter 23 — The Fairness Standard

### Part 2 of 2 · The Practice

**Part IV · CLOSE**

*Part 1 of 2 covers the standard, when to reach for it, the words, and what good sounds like.*

---

## The drill

**"Honour It Once."** This one runs in the field rather than the room, over a
week.

1. Each rep picks one live deal and delivers a fairness invitation on the next
   call, verbatim from the list.
2. They log the response.
3. **If it gets used, they must honour it** — a real adjustment, however small,
   made on the spot without escalation.
4. In the following week's session, each rep reports what it cost and what
   changed.

The reporting is the drill. What reps discover, reliably, is that the cost of
honouring it is trivial and the change in the relationship is not. Until
someone experiences that, the tool stays theoretical — and a fairness
invitation nobody believes in is worse than none.

**The room-based version, for teams who need it first:** operator delivers the
invitation, counterpart immediately uses it on something significant, operator
must respond without hedging, escalating, or explaining. Score the hedge.

---

## Scored on

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| **Invitation delivered early** | Before terms are on the table. Binary. |
| **Times used** | Count. Zero across a quarter means nobody believes it — check delivery. |
| **Honour rate** | Percentage of uses that produced an actual adjustment. Must be 100%. Anything less and the tool is spent. |
| **Fair defined in their words** | Documented per deal. Binary. |
| **Late-stage surprises** | Objections appearing after verbal agreement. This is the number the chapter exists to reduce — track it before and after. |

The last row is where the argument lives. A floor that runs fairness
invitations properly sees late-stage surprises fall, and late-stage surprises
are the most expensive category of objection there is, because they arrive
after everyone has already spent the effort.

---

## Failure mode

**Offering it and then arguing with it.** They take you up on the invitation —
*"the termination clause feels one-sided"* — and the rep, reflexively, defends
the clause. *"Actually that's pretty standard, most of our clients…"*

The invitation is now worse than worthless. You asked them to tell you when
something felt wrong, they trusted the ask, and you argued. Nothing you offer
for the rest of the negotiation carries weight, and they will not raise the
next thing.

**What it looks like on tape:** a fairness invitation, then a use of it, then
the word "actually" or "standard."

**The recovery:** stop mid-defence and honour it.

```
  "I just did the exact thing I told you I wouldn't. You
   said it feels one-sided — so let's change it. What
   would balanced look like?"
```

**The second failure mode: using it as a shield.** Some reps deploy the
fairness language and then treat it as having settled the question — *"I did
say to tell me if it felt unfair"* — as though the invitation itself
discharges the obligation. It doesn't. The invitation is a promise to act, and
an unredeemed promise is a debt.
