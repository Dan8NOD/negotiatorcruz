## The drill

**"Shut Up and Score."** Twenty minutes. Pairs. This is the least popular drill
in the manual and the one that moves numbers fastest.

1. Pair up. One person is the operator, one is the counterpart. The
   counterpart brings a **real** situation from their own work, not invented,
   because invented problems are too tidy and don't have the fuzzy words in
   them.
2. The operator gets **six minutes** and a hard constraint: they may only use
   minimal encouragers, Listen-For lines, and Clean Language questions. No
   solutions. No opinions. No "we could probably, ". Nothing that starts with
   "I."
3. A third person times it and marks the operator's talk time with a stopwatch,
   thumb down when the operator speaks, thumb up when they stop.
4. At six minutes, the operator has one job: **state the counterpart's position
   back, in one paragraph, accurately enough that the counterpart says "yeah,
   that's it."**
5. Swap. Then compare talk-time numbers around the room.

The first round is uncomfortable and the room will be loud about it. Someone
will say it felt unnatural. It did. Someone will say they had to physically
stop themselves three times. Good, now they know the reflex exists, which
they didn't before lunch.

Run this in week one of any rollout. It establishes the talk-time baseline
that everything in Chapter 31 is measured against, and it's the drill people
remember six months later.

---

## Scored on

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| **Talk-time ratio** | Under 30% in a diagnostic conversation. Under 40% anywhere in Stages 1-3. Measured, not estimated. The estimate is always wrong by about twenty points. |
| **Fuzzy words converted** | Count the vague words the counterpart used. Count how many got made specific. Under half means the operator was assuming. |
| **Playback accuracy** | The counterpart agrees with the operator's summary without amending it. Binary, and brutal. |
| **Encouragers that ended** | An encourager followed by more talking from the operator doesn't count. Score the clean ones only. |
| **Solutions offered** | Zero, in Stage 1. Any number above zero is a Stage-1 failure regardless of how good the solution was. |

Talk time is the number to put on a wall. It's objective, it's cheap to
measure, everyone believes theirs is fine, and watching it fall from 68% to 41%
over a month is the most persuasive thing a skeptical floor will ever see.

## Failure mode

**Listening to reply.** The universal one. You are not silent, you are
loading, assembling a rebuttal while arranging your face into an interested
shape. And here is the part people underestimate: **it is visible.** The
counterpart can't articulate what tipped them off, but they register it, and
they start giving you the shorter version of everything.

**What it looks like on tape:** the operator's response begins before the
counterpart's last word finishes. Milliseconds. That overlap is the tell, and
once you've heard it in your own recording you'll never unhear it.

**The recovery:** admit the drift and hand the floor back. It costs nothing and
buys a surprising amount.

```
  "I got ahead of you there. Go back. You were saying
   something about the timeline."
```

Naming the specific thing they were saying proves you were at least partly
present, which is why this works better than a generic "sorry, go on."

**The second failure mode: the sympathetic nod.** This one is harder to catch
because it looks like the tool working. The operator listens warmly, encourages
beautifully, never interrupts, and never asks a Clean Language question,
because pressing on a vague word feels rude. They finish the call feeling like
it went well and holding no usable information. Warmth without precision is
just a nice conversation. You can have those on your own time.
