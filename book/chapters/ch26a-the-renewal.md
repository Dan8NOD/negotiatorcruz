# Chapter 26: The Renewal and the Price Increase

### Part 1 of 2 · The Method

**Part V · APPLICATIONS**

---

## The Standard

A renewal is not a negotiation that starts sixty days out. It is a negotiation
you have been conducting, unaware, for the entire contract term.

Every support ticket that took too long, every invoice that arrived larger than
expected, every promised feature that slipped, every quarterly review someone
scheduled and then moved. All of it was position-building. By the time you
open the renewal conversation, the other side is not forming a view. They are
consulting one they've been assembling since month two.

This is Chapter 1's principle at its most extreme, and it produces the
operating standard: **the renewal negotiation is won or lost in the delivery
period, and the conversation sixty days out is a reading exercise, not a
persuasion one.**

The price increase makes it harder in a specific way. You are asking for more
money in exchange for the same thing, which is a genuinely weak position, and
pretending otherwise is the fastest way to lose the room. The tool for a weak
position stated honestly is the accusation audit.

---

## When to reach for it

- **Ninety days out, not sixty.** The extra month is for Read, not for
  negotiating. You need the delivery history before you need the meeting.
- **Immediately after anything goes wrong.** A bad escalation is a renewal
  event. Handle it as one.
- **When they go quiet.** Silence from an existing account is the most
  misread signal in commercial work. Happy customers are also quiet. The two
  look identical until the renewal lands.

Do **not**:

- **Open with the increase.** Chapter 10 first. Always.
- **Treat incumbency as leverage.** It is switching cost, which is real but
  finite, and it is the only asset you have that depreciates every time
  something goes wrong.
- **Assume the champion is still there.** People move. Verify before you build
  a plan around a name.

## The words

### First: read the term you just delivered

```
LOCATING THE RENEWAL

  "Looking back over the year, what's the thing we got
   most wrong?"
  "If you were writing our review internally, what would
   the criticism be?"
  "What's the one thing that would have made this year
   easier on your side?"
  "Who inside your organization has been least happy
   with us?"
  "What's changed on your end since we signed?"
```

*"Who inside your organization has been least happy with us?"* is the important
one. There is always someone, they are often not your contact, and they
will be in the room where this gets decided (Chapter 9).

### Then: the increase, front-loaded

The accusation audit does the work here because every objection to a price
increase is one you already know.

```
THE INCREASE, audit first

  "You're probably thinking we're charging more for exactly
   the same thing."
  "It might look like we waited until you were locked in
   to do this."
  "You're likely wondering why this is our problem to solve
   and not ours to absorb."
  "It probably feels like the timing is convenient for us
   and not for you."
```

Then the mechanism, plainly, and then stop:

```
  "Here's the actual reason, and you can tell me if it's
   not good enough."
```

### Leverage, know which kind you actually have

Most renewal conversations fail because the rep reaches for the only leverage
they've ever named. There are three:

| Type | What it is | In a renewal |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | You have something they want | New capability, better terms, roadmap access |
| **Negative** | You can cost them something they fear losing | Migration cost, retraining, data extraction, the quarter it would eat |
| **Normative** | Their own stated standards, applied to an inconsistency | "You told us predictability mattered more than price" |

Negative leverage is usually the strongest, because people work harder to avoid
a loss than to chase a gain, and in a renewal it is almost always *theirs* to
lose, not yours. **It is also the one to name least directly.** A migration
cost you describe is a threat. A migration cost they compute, in answer to a
loss question from Chapter 20, is a fact.

Normative leverage is the most underused and the safest. If they told you
eighteen months ago that reliability mattered more than price, and they are now
arguing price, that inconsistency is theirs to resolve, not yours to attack.

## What good sounds like

**A criticism you hadn't heard.** The point of the ninety-day read. A
complaint volunteered at day ninety is a gift. The same complaint at day thirty
is an objection.

**"Honestly the increase isn't the issue."** Extremely common, and it means the
increase was never the negotiation. Something else is, and you now get to find
out what.

**They compute the switching cost out loud.** Chapter 20's loss family working.
Never say it for them.

**A name you didn't have.** Chapter 9. Renewal committees are wider than
purchase committees, because by now finance, operations, and security all have
opinions.

**What good does not sound like:** enthusiastic agreement with no process
detail. A renewal that sounds easy in month ten and goes to procurement in
month eleven was never easy.
