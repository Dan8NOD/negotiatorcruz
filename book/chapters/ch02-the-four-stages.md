# Chapter 2 — The Four Stages

**Part 0 · THE STANDARD**

---

## The Standard

Every negotiation runs through four stages, in this order, every time:

> **Read → Disarm → Steer → Close**

You diagnose what is actually happening. You defuse the resistance that is
already loaded. You move them with questions rather than pressure. Then you
stop talking and let them close.

The order is not a preference and it is not a style. It is the operating
constraint of the entire method. Each stage produces the input the next one
requires: you cannot defuse resistance you haven't identified, you cannot
steer someone who is still defending, and you cannot close a person who hasn't
arrived at the answer themselves. Run out of order, the tools still fire — they
just fire into a situation that isn't ready for them, which is worse than not
firing at all.

This is the whole method. The remaining 31 chapters are instruction on the
individual tools inside these four stages, plus what to do when the stages meet
real conditions.

---

## Why the order is fixed

Crisis negotiators arrived at a fixed sequence for a reason that has nothing to
do with elegance: **a sequence works when the person running it is exhausted,
frightened, or new.** A judgment call doesn't. When the cost of a wrong move is
immediate and irreversible, you do not want a practitioner improvising an order
of operations — you want them running the same sequence they have run four
hundred times.

Commercial negotiation has lower stakes and identical ergonomics. Your rep is
on their fourth call of the morning, the deal is bad, and they didn't sleep.
Under that load, people do not execute frameworks. They execute habits. The
four stages are built to be habitual, which is why they are the same four, in
the same order, in every deal, in every vertical, regardless of size.

Here is what each stage produces and what it costs to skip it.

### Stage 1 — READ · *diagnose*

**Produces:** an accurate read of the emotional state and the decision
structure. Who is in this, what they're feeling, what they're actually
protecting.

**Skip it and:** every subsequent move is aimed at a problem you invented.
The most common version is a rep who solves a budget objection for three weeks
while the real obstacle is a stakeholder who was overruled in March and is
quietly waiting to be proven right.

**Tools:** the emotion vocabulary, labels, mirrors, tactical silence, reading
decision structure. Chapters 4–9.

### Stage 2 — DISARM · *defuse*

**Produces:** resistance that has been named out loud and can no longer be
deployed against you.

**Skip it and:** you steer into a loaded gun. Every unspoken objection —
you're too expensive, you'll disappear after signing, we've done this before
and it didn't work — stays live and gets used at the worst possible moment,
usually after you've already made your case and have nothing left to answer
with.

**Tools:** the accusation audit, naming your own weakness first, no-oriented
questions, validation, the discipline of not defending. Chapters 10–14.

### Stage 3 — STEER · *redirect*

**Produces:** movement the other side owns, because they generated it.

**Skip it and:** you're presenting. Presenting produces compliance at best and
resistance at worst, and neither survives the buyer's next internal
conversation — the one you're not in, where your argument has to be repeated
by someone else. Answers people arrive at themselves get repeated accurately.
Answers you gave them do not.

**Tools:** calibrated questions, the How/What grammar, summaries, trading,
the Ackerman system, deadline dynamics. Chapters 15–20.

### Stage 4 — CLOSE · *resolve*

**Produces:** agreement, arrived at rather than extracted.

**Skip it and:** you have done the hard work and then talked past the moment
they were ready. This is the most common expensive failure on an experienced
floor, and it is almost always a rep filling a silence.

**Tools:** no-oriented questions, the "that's right" summary, reframing, the
six-second hold. Chapters 21–25.

## The Levels are a different axis

The four Stages describe **what you run inside a negotiation.** They say
nothing about how good you are at running it.

That is the second axis, and it is the one the training program is organized
around:

| | **Level 1 — Listening** | **Level 2 — Dialogue** | **Level 3 — Collaboration** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Competence** | Keep the conversation open. Hear what is actually said. | Surface constraints. Move someone without pressure. | Reframe the problem. Hold the room under load. |
| **Stages in play** | Read | Read → Disarm → Steer | All four, under pressure |
| **Certified when** | Labels land, silence held, no solving before diagnosing | Calibrated questions run clean, talk time under 40% | Full sequence executed on a hostile recorded rep |

**Stages are the sequence. Levels are the ladder.** A Level 1 operator runs
Stage 1 well and hands off. A Level 3 operator runs all four alone in a room
with a hostile committee. Both are running the same Protocol.

This distinction matters commercially, not just pedagogically. It is what
allows a manager to be **certified to run the Protocol on their own floor** —
scoring reps, running the drills, signing off on levels — without the author
in the building. Chapters 31–33 are the installation instructions for exactly
that.

## The words

The transitions matter more than most people expect, because a stage change
that the other side can hear reads as technique. These are the sentences that
move you between stages without announcing it.

```
STAGE TRANSITIONS

  READ → DISARM
  After the label lands. You have the diagnosis; now name
  the objection before they do.
  "Before we go further, let me say the part you're
   probably already thinking."

  DISARM → STEER
  After the objection has been named and you have NOT
  defended it. Hand them the problem.
  "So given all of that — how would you want to
   approach this?"

  STEER → CLOSE
  After they've done the work and you can summarize their
  position better than they stated it.
  "Let me make sure I have this right."
  [summary]  →  wait for "that's right"  →  stop.
```

Notice what is absent. There is no transition sentence that contains your
offer, your price, or your case. Those exist — they just don't live in the
transitions. If your movement between stages requires you to present
something, you are not changing stages, you are pitching in four parts.

---

## What good sounds like

You are allowed to advance a stage when the stage has produced its output —
not when you're ready, and not when the clock says so.

**Cleared to leave Read:** you can state, out loud, what they're feeling and
who else is in the decision, and you have evidence for both. If you can't name
three participants (Chapter 1) and one specific emotion (Chapter 5), you are
still in Stage 1 regardless of how long you've been talking.

**Cleared to leave Disarm:** you have said the worst thing out loud and not
defended it, and the temperature dropped rather than rose. A rise means the
audit was aimed at the wrong objection. Go back to Read.

**Cleared to leave Steer:** they have proposed something. Anything. The moment
the other side starts constructing rather than evaluating, Stage 3 has done its
job.

**Cleared to close:** they say "that's right" to your summary — not "you're
right," which is appeasement (Chapter 6), and not "exactly," which is often
politeness. "That's right" is the sound of someone who feels understood, and it
is the only reliable green light in the method.

## The drill

**"Call the Stage."** Twenty minutes, weekly, run by a manager with a laptop
and one recording.

1. Play a recorded call from your own pipeline. Not a demo, not a
   best-of — an ordinary one.
2. Every thirty seconds, pause. Everyone writes down which stage the rep is
   **actually in** — not which one they intended.
3. Around the table on each pause. Disagreements are the useful part: two
   people hearing different stages means the rep didn't signal a transition.
4. At the end, map it. Most recordings produce something like:
   `READ (40s) → STEER → STEER → STEER → CLOSE → STEER → STEER`

That map is the diagnosis. A floor that jumps from a 40-second Read straight
to Steer and then oscillates has one problem, and it is not a skills problem —
it is a sequence problem, and it is fixable in a day.

Run this before you run any tool-specific drill. There is no value in
sharpening a calibrated question that is being deployed in the wrong stage.

## Scored on

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| **Sequence integrity** | Stages appear in order, with no stage skipped. Score per call, binary per stage. |
| **Read duration** | Stage 1 occupies at least 25% of total call time. Under 15% means the rep is diagnosing on the way past. |
| **Disarm present at all** | Binary. On most untrained floors this stage is simply absent — the single highest-yield thing to measure in week one. |
| **Talk-time ratio in Steer** | Under 40%. Above it, the rep is presenting, not steering. |
| **Concessions after silence** | Zero. Anything conceded after the other side went quiet is a Stage 4 failure. |

The second and third rows are where a new floor moves fastest. Most teams have
some version of Read and Close already, badly. Almost none of them have Disarm
at all — which means it's the cheapest measurable improvement available, and
it's the one leadership can see inside two weeks.

---

## Failure mode

**Jumping straight to Steer.** This is the single most common failure in the
field, it happens on experienced floors more than new ones, and it is almost
never laziness. It's urgency. The rep hears a problem, feels the clock, and
skips to the part where they fix it — because Steer is the stage that *feels*
like negotiating. Read feels like small talk. Disarm feels like volunteering
weakness. Steer feels like work.

So they arrive at Stage 3 with no diagnosis and a live objection sitting
underneath the conversation, and they start asking calibrated questions into a
person who is still defending. The questions land as interrogation. The rep
concludes the tool doesn't work.

**What it looks like on tape:** a question mark in the rep's first two
sentences that isn't a locating question, or the phrase "so what if we—" inside
the first three minutes.

**The recovery:** stop steering and go back to Stage 1 out loud. It costs
thirty seconds and it works from anywhere in the call.

```
  "I've been throwing solutions at this and I don't think
   I've actually understood where you're sitting yet.
   What's the part of this that's hardest for you?"
```

**The second failure mode:** treating the stages as time blocks rather than
gates. Four stages does not mean four equal quarters. A renewal with a hostile
stakeholder can spend 80% of a call in Read and be executed perfectly. The
gates are the outputs listed above — not the clock.
