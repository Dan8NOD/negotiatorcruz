## The Standard

Validation is telling the other side that their position makes sense given
where they're standing.

It is not agreement. That distinction is the whole chapter, and it is the
reason most commercial negotiators avoid the tool: they believe that
acknowledging the reasonableness of an objection concedes it. It doesn't.
**"That makes complete sense" and "I'll do something about it" are different
sentences, and you can say the first indefinitely without ever saying the
second.**

What validation buys is the precondition for movement. A person defending their
position cannot evaluate yours — the defending consumes the capacity. Validate
the position and the defending stops, because there is nothing left to defend
against. Only then is there room to steer.

Three instruments live here, and they answer three different problems: the
empathy statement (their reaction is disproportionate to the facts), the
positive label (their self-image needs somewhere to stand), and trust repair
(they've been burned and your word isn't currency).

---

## When to reach for it

Reach for it when:

- **Logic has stopped working.** The shipped trigger for the empathy family
  says it precisely: *their logic won't move them — their feelings will.* If
  you have answered the stated objection twice and it keeps returning, you are
  not in an argument about facts.
- **They're braced.** Someone waiting to be talked out of something cannot
  hear anything else until the bracing stops.
- **They've been burned.** Not by you, usually. Most distrust in a commercial
  negotiation is inherited from whoever had the contract before you.
- **They're embarrassed about their own position.** People defend hardest what
  they're least comfortable holding. Validation removes the shame and
  frequently the position goes with it.

Do **not** reach for it when:

- **You're about to follow it with "but."** "That makes complete sense, but—"
  is not validation, it is a preamble, and everyone recognizes the shape.
- **It isn't true.** Manufactured validation is detectable and expensive.
  Validate something you actually find reasonable — and if nothing about their
  position is reasonable, use a label instead and stop pretending.
- **They haven't shown you anything to validate.** Validating a position that
  hasn't been stated is flattery aimed at a target that isn't there.

## The words

### Empathy statements — the reaction is proportionate

```
EMPATHY

  "That reaction makes complete sense given what's on the line."
  "That's not an overreaction. That's what this situation calls for."
  "Anyone in your position would be asking the same questions right now."
  "It seems like this feels personal, not just practical."
  "You've clearly been carrying this longer than just today's
   conversation."
  "You're doing more processing here than you're getting credit for."
  "It's fair to be careful here — the last version of this didn't
   go well."
  "You're allowed to still be deciding. This isn't a small thing."
  "It makes sense you'd want more than my word on this."
  "Nobody sits with something like this over nothing. Something real
   is behind it."
```

*"That's not an overreaction. That's what this situation calls for."* is the
strongest line in the family. People who have become emotional in a business
setting are almost always managing a second layer of discomfort about having
become emotional. That sentence removes the second layer, and the first one
often subsides on its own once it isn't being policed.

### Positive labels — give them somewhere to stand

```
POSITIVE LABELS

  "You seem like the type of person who prides themselves on how
   they do business."
  "It seems like you'd like to make this work, but there are things
   in the way."
  "It sounds like you're someone who follows through on what you say."
  "It seems like you actually care how this turns out for both of us."
  "It sounds like you've given this a lot of thought."
  "It looks like you came prepared — that's not nothing."
  "It looks like you've done this before — that helps."
  "It seems like you're genuinely trying to get this right."
  "Feels like you're being really generous with your time here."
  "It seems like you actually want this to work."
  "It sounds like you're the kind of person who makes this easy."
  "Sounds like we're in good hands here."
```

Two rules. **It must be true** — an untrue positive label is flattery and lands
as manipulation, which costs you more than the label was worth. And **it works
by consistency, not by flattery**: a person who has just been described as
someone who prides themselves on how they do business now has a description to
live up to, and most people quietly do. That is not a trick, provided the
description is one you actually believe. If it isn't, don't say it.

*"It seems like you'd like to make this work, but there are things in the way"*
is the most useful of the twelve, because it separates the person from the
obstacle. They are no longer the problem. The problem is the problem, and the
two of you are now looking at it from the same side.

### Trust repair — when your word isn't currency

These are questions rather than statements, because trust is not restored by
assertion. The shipped trigger is exact: *they've been burned before. Proof,
not promises.*

```
TRUST REPAIR

  "At what point did trust break down here?"
  "Who let you down the last time something like this came up?"
  "How many times has this promise been made before and not kept?"
  "What's the story you're not telling me about why this feels risky?"
  "If this goes the way you're afraid it will, what happened
   right before that?"
  "What would it take to actually trust this, not just agree to it?"
  "What would proof look like here, if my word alone isn't enough?"
  "What's the smallest thing I could do right now that would
   mean something?"
  "What would need to be different this time for it to actually
   be different?"
  "How do we rebuild trust from where we are?"
  "Who's the one person whose word you'd actually take on this?"
  "What's the fastest way I could lose whatever trust we've built
   so far?"
```

*"What's the smallest thing I could do right now that would mean something?"*
is the operational one. Trust is rebuilt in small verifiable increments and
never in one large promise, and this question asks them to specify the
increment. Whatever they name, do it, and do it faster than they expect.

*"What's the fastest way I could lose whatever trust we've built so far?"* is
the most underused sentence in this manual. It gets you their exact tripwires,
volunteered, in advance — and it signals that you expect to be held to them.

## What good sounds like

**They stop defending.** The primary signal, and it is physical before it is
verbal. Shoulders, pace, sentence length. Chapter 5's somatic reads.

**"Exactly — and the other thing is…"** Validation reliably produces
elaboration. A person who feels their position is understood will extend it,
and the extension is where the useful material lives.

**A concession you didn't ask for.** Frequent enough to be worth expecting.
People who feel validated often soften their own position unprompted, because
the position was partly a defensive structure and the threat has gone.

**They tell you the story.** Trust-repair questions produce narratives — the
previous vendor, the implementation that went wrong, the person who left. Let
it run. The story contains their entire decision criteria.

**What good does not sound like:** "thanks, I appreciate that." Polite,
terminal, and a sign that the validation was received as a pleasantry rather
than as recognition. Usually means it was too general — go more specific.
