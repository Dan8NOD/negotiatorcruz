## The Standard

An asking label gets you the answer without asking the question.

You state what you believe to be true, as an observation, and let them confirm
or correct it. No question mark. No request. The shipped trigger names the
situation precisely: **you need an answer, but a direct question would put them
on guard.**

That situation is more common than the alternative. Direct questions in a
commercial negotiation carry a cost that nobody accounts for — they announce
what you want to know, which tells the other side what matters to you, which is
information you have now given away for free. "Do you have another quote?"
tells them you're worried about competition. "It sounds like you're comparing
this against something else you're weighing" gets the same answer and tells
them nothing.

This is the last tool in the Read family and the first in Steer, which is why
it sits where it does. It diagnoses like a label and it moves like a question.

---

## When to reach for it

Reach for it when:

- **The direct question would reveal your concern.** Anything about
  competitors, budget ceilings, deadlines, or authority. Ask those directly and
  you've published your anxieties.
- **You've already asked and got nothing.** A question that produced a
  non-answer will produce a non-answer again. Change instrument, not volume.
- **They're guarded.** A guarded counterpart deflects questions well and
  corrects inaccuracies badly — that asymmetry is the entire opening.
- **You're fairly confident and want confirmation.** Asking labels are strongest
  when your read is 70% right. Too certain and there's nothing to correct; too
  wrong and it's a mislabel (Chapter 9), which is a different tool with a
  different purpose.

Do **not** reach for it when:

- **A direct question costs you nothing.** Don't be clever about logistics.
  "What's your fiscal year end?" is a fine question and dressing it up as a
  label is affectation.
- **You have no read at all.** An asking label with nothing behind it is a
  guess, and a wrong guess spends credibility you'll want later.
- **You've used two already.** Like every label family: one, then silence.

## The words

```
ASKING LABELS — position and intent

  "It sounds like you've already decided which way you're leaning."
  "It seems like you'd rather keep this simple than get everything
   perfect."
  "It sounds like the real sticking point isn't the one we're
   talking about."
  "It seems like you'd say yes today if the one thing holding you
   back got solved."

ASKING LABELS — the number

  "It seems like there's a number in mind, even if it hasn't been
   said yet."
  "It sounds like the timeline matters more to you than the price does."

ASKING LABELS — the alternative

  "It sounds like you're comparing this against something else
   you're weighing."
  "It seems like you already know what you'd do if this fell through."

ASKING LABELS — authority and urgency

  "It seems like this needs a yes from someone else in the room first."
  "It sounds like there's a reason this can't wait much longer."
```

Look at what each of those replaces.

| The question you'd have asked | What it tells them | The asking label |
|---|---|---|
| "Are you talking to other vendors?" | You're worried about competition | "It sounds like you're comparing this against something else you're weighing." |
| "What's your budget?" | You want to price to their ceiling | "It seems like there's a number in mind, even if it hasn't been said yet." |
| "Do you need someone else to approve this?" | You doubt their authority | "It seems like this needs a yes from someone else in the room first." |
| "What's your deadline?" | You're looking for pressure to use | "It sounds like there's a reason this can't wait much longer." |

Same information, opposite disclosure. In every row the question broadcasts
your strategy and the label conceals it — while producing a *fuller* answer,
because a correction always carries more detail than a reply.

Two lines deserve individual attention.

**"It seems like you'd say yes today if the one thing holding you back got
solved."** The most commercially useful sentence in Part III. It presumes there
is exactly one obstacle, which flatters the deal into being simpler than it is,
and it invites them to name it. Most of the time they do — and if the answer is
"it's not one thing, it's three," you've just been handed the list.

**"It sounds like the real sticking point isn't the one we're talking about."**
Deploy this when a negotiation has been circling the same objection for two
meetings. It is almost always true, and saying it out loud is frequently the
only thing that will get the conversation off the decoy.

## What good sounds like

**A correction with the answer inside it.** *"It's not another vendor — it's
that we're deciding whether to do it in-house."* You proposed a competitor,
they gave you the real alternative, and it's a more useful one.

**A confirmation plus an extension.** *"There is a number, yeah — and honestly
it's not one I set."* Two facts for one sentence: a budget exists, and Chapter
9's decision structure just opened up.

**A pause and then honesty.** Asking labels frequently produce the *"look, I'll
be straight with you"* register shift. That register is the whole game and it
almost never arrives in response to a direct question.

**Silence, then a change of subject.** Not a failure. It means you were right
and they didn't want to confirm it. Note it and proceed as though confirmed —
carefully.

**What good does not sound like:** a bare "yeah." Agreement with no elaboration
means the label was too safe to be worth correcting. Aim closer to the bone
next time.
