## The Standard

Before you lock it, you ask them what's missing.

*"Before we sign — what did I miss that would make this better?"*

Every instinct says don't. You are at the finish line, agreement is in hand,
and you are about to invite a new request into a closed deal. It reads as
reckless.

It isn't, and the reason is that **the ask you invite is cheap and the ask you
suppress is expensive.** Everyone comes out of a negotiation holding something
they didn't raise — a small thing, usually, that they decided wasn't worth
reopening the conversation for. That unraised thing does not evaporate when the
contract is signed. It becomes the first item of the implementation, the tone
of the first support ticket, and a quiet deduction from how good the deal felt.

Ask for it at the close and it costs you almost nothing, because at that moment
they are motivated to keep it small. Leave it unasked and it comes back later,
larger, at a point where you have no leverage left and no goodwill to spend.

---

## When to reach for it

Reach for it when:

- **Agreement is reached but not executed.** The window is after "yes" and
  before signature. That's the whole slot.
- **You've had a "that's right."** Chapter 22 first. This tool assumes they
  feel understood, or the question reads as a stalling tactic.
- **The relationship continues.** Renewals, implementations, anything with a
  second act. The value scales with how long you'll be working together.
- **They've been notably accommodating.** An easy counterpart is very often a
  counterpart holding several unraised items. Politeness is not the same as
  satisfaction.

Do **not** reach for it when:

- **The deal is fragile.** If agreement is barely held together, don't invite
  new items into it. Close, then ask at the next natural opportunity.
- **You cannot say yes to anything.** Asking what would make it better, and
  then refusing all of it, is worse than not asking. Know your small change
  before you open the drawer.
- **They've already asked for one more thing.** Then you're trading (Chapter
  18), not sweeping.

## The words

```
ONE MORE THING — the direct sweep

  "Before we sign — what did I miss that would make this better?"
  "Before we call it done, is there a loose end we haven't tied off?"
  "Is there anything you'd want in this that we haven't covered?"
  "Last chance before we lock it in — what's on your mind?"

ONE MORE THING — invite the small want

  "If you could add one thing to this agreement, what would it be?"
  "If this deal could include one more thing at no extra cost,
   what would you ask for?"
  "Is there something small that would make this feel like a win,
   not just a deal?"
  "What would take this from good to actually great for you?"

ONE MORE THING — the thing they held back

  "What's the thing you almost brought up but didn't?"
  "Anything you're quietly hoping gets added before we finalize?"
  "Now that we're basically there, what's the one thing that
   would make this feel complete?"
```

**"What's the thing you almost brought up but didn't?"** is the sharpest of the
eleven, and it works because it presupposes the thing exists. There is always
one. Asking *whether* there's something invites a polite "no"; asking *what it
was* invites an answer.

**"Is there something small that would make this feel like a win, not just a
deal?"** does something worth noticing — it distinguishes between the
transaction and how the transaction is experienced, and it explicitly asks
about the second. Buyers rarely get asked how a deal *feels*, and the answer is
frequently something that costs you nothing: a named contact, an earlier
kickoff, an introduction, their logo left off a case study, their team's
timeline respected in week one.

**"If this deal could include one more thing at no extra cost, what would you
ask for?"** front-loads the constraint. You've told them the budget before they
answer, so what comes back is already scoped. This is the safest version of the
question and the one to start with if the sweep makes you nervous.

Note what is absent from all eleven: any version of "are you happy?" That
question gets "yes" from everyone, including people who aren't, and it is the
reason most closing check-ins produce nothing.

## What good sounds like

**A small, specific ask.** *"Honestly? I'd just want the kickoff after the
15th, our team's underwater until then."* Costs you a calendar change. Buys
you a counterpart who starts the relationship having been listened to.

**A relieved laugh, then the thing.** The most common shape. People are
slightly embarrassed to have been holding something trivial, and the
embarrassment is the tell that it was genuinely unraised.

**"No, honestly, I think we're good."** Sometimes true, and it costs you eight
seconds to have checked. It also lands as confidence — only someone who
believes the deal is good asks whether it could be better.

**Something structural you'd have hit anyway.** *"Well — who's actually running
the implementation?"* Not a want, a gap. Far better discovered now than in week
two.

**What good does not sound like:** a large new demand. If the sweep produces a
price reopening, the agreement wasn't as closed as you thought, and you've
learned something important. Go back to Chapter 22 — you probably had a
"you're right" rather than a "that's right."
