# Chapter 7 — Mirroring

**Part I · READ**

---

## The Standard

A mirror is the last one to three words the other side said, repeated back to
them with a slight upward inflection, followed by nothing.

That's it. That's the whole tool.

It should not work. On paper it reads as the least sophisticated technique
available to a professional — you are, functionally, doing the thing toddlers
do to irritate their siblings. And yet it is the most reliable information
extractor in Part I, it works on people who know exactly what you're doing, and
it works in every language anyone has tested it in.

The mechanism is boring, which is why people don't trust it. A mirror is not a
question, so it doesn't trigger the defensive posture that questions do. It's
not a statement, so there's nothing to disagree with. It leaves an unfinished
shape in the air, and human beings are extremely bad at leaving unfinished
shapes alone. So they fill it — and what they use to fill it is elaboration
they had no intention of giving you.

---

## When to reach for it

Reach for a mirror when:

- **They say something loaded and you want more of it.** "Impossible,"
  "ridiculous," "unfair," "not interested" — every one of those is a door with
  something behind it.
- **A number lands.** Mirroring a number back is the cheapest way to make
  someone justify it, and they will, unprompted, at length.
- **You're at risk of arguing.** A mirror is the pressure-release valve. It
  buys you four seconds and costs you no ground, because you haven't taken a
  position.
- **You don't know what to say.** Genuinely. This is a legitimate use. The
  mirror is what a professional does instead of filling silence with something
  they'll regret.

Do **not** mirror when:

- **You've already mirrored in the last minute or two.** Two in quick
  succession is noticeable, and the second one is where it stops being invisible
  and starts being a technique. Three is a parody.
- **They asked you a direct, factual question.** Mirroring "what's your
  implementation timeline?" back at someone is not clever, it's evasive, and
  they will read it correctly as evasion.
- **The word you'd be mirroring is emotionally enormous.** Mirroring
  "bankruptcy" or "my father died" is not a negotiation move. Use a label
  (Chapter 6), or use nothing.

---

## The words

There isn't much to memorize, which is the point — the whole tool has to
survive being executed by someone under pressure.

```
THE MIRROR

  Repeat their last three words, then stop talking.
  Echo the last word of their sentence like a question, and let it sit.
  Take their strongest word and hand it right back to them.
  Catch the word that carried the most weight in that sentence and
   echo it — nothing else.
  Pick the word they leaned on hardest and give it right back to them.
  Take the last thing they said and turn it into a question with your
   tone alone.
```

```
WORKED EXAMPLES

  Them: "...it's really a budget issue this quarter."
  You:  "A budget issue this quarter?"

  Them: "We're not interested."
  You:  "Not interested?"

  Them: "Honestly, the whole thing is just impossible right now."
  You:  "Impossible?"

  Them: "We'd need it under sixty thousand."
  You:  "Sixty thousand?"
```

Three rules govern all of it.

**Upward inflection, not flat.** Flat is a statement and sounds like
disagreement. Up is curiosity. This is the single most common execution error
and it is entirely fixable by hearing yourself do it once.

**Nothing after.** No "…is it?", no "…tell me more," no nervous laugh. The
mirror ends and then you are quiet. Everything that follows a mirror weakens it
in proportion to its length.

**Mirror the number, not your reaction to the number.** When someone says a
figure that's insulting, there are two available responses: the mirror, or the
face. Do the mirror. Your reaction is a concession you're giving away for free
— they learn where your line is and you learn nothing.

Two lines from the catalog earn special mention:

> *"If they use a loaded word — 'impossible,' 'ridiculous,' 'unfair' — hand it
> straight back."*

Loaded words are where people have compressed a story into one adjective.
Mirroring decompresses it. Nine times out of ten the story that comes back out
is more specific, less absolute, and considerably more negotiable than the word
suggested.

> *"Say the number, then let the room go quiet. Don't soften it after."*

Filed under mirrors because it's the same discipline pointed at yourself.
Whatever you said last is now doing work. Talking again takes it back.

---

## What good sounds like

**They keep going.** The most common outcome and the reason the tool exists.
You get another twenty to forty seconds of unplanned talking, and unplanned
talking is where constraints live.

**The absolute becomes conditional.** "Impossible?" → *"Well — not impossible,
but we'd have to move it into next year's budget, and I'd have to sell that to
Rachel."* You now have a timeline, an obstacle, and a name. Three pieces of
intelligence for one word.

**They restate it more precisely.** People often mirror-check themselves. "A
budget issue?" → *"Well, it's not the budget exactly, it's that the budget's
already been allocated."* Not the same thing at all, and now you know it.

**They laugh.** Occasionally someone clocks the technique and says so. This is
fine and it isn't a failure. Say "fair enough," smile, and ask the real
question — the four seconds of rapport you just gained are worth more than the
mirror was.

**What good does not sound like:** them repeating the same words back at you
with an edge. That's the sound of a mirror used as a stall when they wanted an
answer. Stop mirroring, answer the question.

---

## The drill

**"The Last Three Words."** Ten minutes. Pairs. The fastest drill in the
manual and the one most likely to produce laughter, which is useful — the tool
requires you to be comfortable looking slightly ridiculous.

1. Pairs. The counterpart talks about a real work problem for two minutes.
2. The operator's **only** permitted contributions are mirrors. Nothing else.
   No encouragers, no questions, no "mm-hmm."
3. A timer runs. The operator must mirror **at least four times** in two
   minutes — which is deliberately too many, and that's the lesson.
4. Swap. Then discuss one thing only: **at which point did it start to feel
   like a technique?** Everyone's answer is between the third and fourth. That's
   your natural ceiling — in live work, halve it.

**The advanced variant, for anyone who thinks they've got it:** the operator
must mirror only the single most loaded word in each of the counterpart's
statements — not the last words, the *heaviest* one. That's a materially harder
skill, it's the version that actually gets used in the field, and it's what
separates a Level 2 operator from a Level 1 one.

---

## Scored on

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| **Clean delivery** | One to three words, upward inflection, nothing after. Binary per mirror. |
| **Elaboration rate** | Percentage of mirrors that produce further talking. Above 70% is competent. Below 50% means flat delivery — check the inflection first, it's almost always the inflection. |
| **Density** | No more than one mirror per two minutes in live work. Over-use scores as a failure. |
| **Loaded-word selection** | Did the operator mirror the heaviest word, or just the last one? Score as a separate line — this is the Level 2 skill. |
| **Reaction leakage** | Zero audible reactions to numbers. A sharp inhale before a mirror cancels the mirror. |

---

## Failure mode

**Mirroring into a wall.** The operator mirrors, gets nothing, panics, and
mirrors again. Now it's obviously a technique, the counterpart has noticed, and
the room has cooled by several degrees.

**What it looks like on tape:** two mirrors within thirty seconds, the second
one delivered at higher pitch than the first.

**The recovery:** stop mirroring and say something with content in it.

```
  "Let me be more direct — what's actually driving the
   sixty-thousand number?"
```

Abandoning a tool that isn't working is a skill in itself, and it is trained
much less often than the tools are.

**The second failure mode: mirroring instead of thinking.** The tool is
comfortable. It requires no preparation, takes no position, and can be run
indefinitely — which makes it an excellent place to hide. An operator who
mirrors their way through a forty-minute call has extracted a great deal of
information and has still not negotiated anything. Mirrors are Stage 1. There
are three more stages, and none of them run themselves.
