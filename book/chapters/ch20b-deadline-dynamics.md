# Chapter 20 — Deadline Dynamics

### Part 2 of 2 · The Practice

**Part III · STEER**

*Part 1 of 2 covers the standard, when to reach for it, the words, and what good sounds like.*

---

## The drill

**"Real or Atmosphere."** Twenty minutes, run against live pipeline.

1. Each rep lists **every deadline currently in their pipeline** — theirs and
   the buyers'.
2. Next to each, write the **mechanism**: the specific thing that happens on
   the day after. Not a consequence in general — the event.
3. Mark each **REAL** (mechanism identified), **UNKNOWN** (never asked), or
   **ATMOSPHERE** (no mechanism exists).
4. For every UNKNOWN, write the urgency-check question to be asked this week.
5. Then reverse it: for each deal, write the buyer's genuine cost of doing
   nothing — and mark whether you actually know it or are assuming it.

The UNKNOWN column is the drill. Most reps are running two or three deals
against deadlines nobody has ever tested, and they've been shaping their
discounting around them.

---

## Scored on

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| **Deadlines tested** | Every counterpart deadline has a documented mechanism or is marked untested. Under 100% means the floor is conceding against fiction. |
| **Atmosphere rate** | Percentage of deadlines that dissolved under the urgency check. Track it — it's usually startling the first quarter. |
| **Concessions to a deadline** | Zero given to any untested deadline. Binary and expensive. |
| **Loss framed as a question** | Zero warnings, zero predictions. All loss aversion delivered as questions the counterpart answers. |
| **Cost of inaction documented** | In the buyer's own words, per deal. Binary. |

---

## Failure mode

**Obeying the clock.** The buyer names a date. The rep believes it, works
backwards from it, and starts making concessions to hit it — a discount to
close before quarter end, a scope change to fit the timeline, an expedited
start nobody costed.

Every one of those was paid to a deadline that was never tested. And the
pattern compounds: a buyer who learns that a date extracts concessions will
supply a date next time, because it worked.

**What it looks like on tape:** the rep repeating the buyer's deadline back as
a shared constraint — *"okay, so we need to get this done by the 15th"* —
without a single question about what the 15th is.

**The recovery:** test it late. Better than never, and it costs less than the
concession you're about to make.

```
  "Before I go rearranging things around the 15th — what
   actually happens on the 16th?"
```

**The second failure mode: using loss aversion as a threat.** The rep, having
learned that inaction has costs, starts announcing them. *"You should know that
prices go up in Q3."* *"I'd hate for you to be in the same position next
year."* Delivered as statements, these are pressure tactics, they are heard as
pressure tactics, and they violate the standard in Chapter 3 — the tools are
for clarity, not confusion. The family works because the other side does the
arithmetic. Do it for them and you've converted a diagnostic into a threat, and
bought yourself a counterpart who is now negotiating against you rather than
with you.
