# Gap Intake — answer these, don't write prose

Twenty-two chapters each need one real case. Writing twenty-two 200-word
vignettes is a miserable job and it is not the job. **Answering questions about
deals you actually ran is a different job, and it's one you can do out loud in
the car.**

So: answer the six questions below for each chapter, in fragments, badly, out
of order. I'll write the prose. What I cannot do is invent the facts, which is
why every one of these sits empty in the draft rather than being filled with
something plausible.

Ranked by `GAP-INDEX.md` priority — top of this list moves the needle most.

---

## The six questions

These turn any war story into a usable case. Same six every time.

1. **Situation.** Who, what industry, roughly what size, what was on the table?
   No names needed — "a mid-size claims operation, six-figure renewal" is
   plenty.
2. **What was said.** The actual sentence, theirs or yours, as close to verbatim
   as you remember. This is the one that matters most and the one people skip.
3. **What you expected.** What you thought was going on before the tool worked.
4. **What actually turned out to be true.** The gap between those two is the
   whole case.
5. **What changed after.** Not the outcome — the *behaviour*. What did you or
   they do differently in the next ten minutes?
6. **Why it worked.** One sentence on the mechanism. If you can't name one, say
   so; that's useful too.

**What makes a case unusable:** a win with no mechanism. "And then they signed"
teaches nothing. A near-miss with a clear mechanism is worth more than a
triumph without one — and for several of these, a **failure is the better
story** (marked below).

## Priority 1 — the evaluator path

These are the chapters Ch 3 tells a buyer to read before spending money. A thin
case here costs you the deal, not just the chapter.

### Ch 2 — The Four Stages
**Need:** a deal that stalled because a stage got skipped, and what changed when
the sequence ran in order.
**Ideally:** the skipped stage was Disarm — that's the one most floors don't
have at all.
**Extra question:** how long was it stalled before anyone noticed the sequence
was wrong?

### Ch 33 — The Manager's Scorecard
**Need:** real inter-rater data. Two managers scoring the same ten tapes is
enough.
**Why it matters more than a story:** this chapter is the audit instrument. A
rubric with no evidence that two people score it the same way is a rubric a
procurement reviewer will dismiss in one question.
**Extra question:** where did the two scorers disagree most, and did you change
the rubric because of it?

## Priority 2 — installation and the one double-gap

### Ch 14 — The Discipline of Not Defending · *two gaps*
**Need A:** confirm the chapter's scope is the Attack Decoder rather than a
broader survey of defending.
**Need B:** three real opening attack lines — one defensive, one from someone
who feels unheard, one purely tactical. Verbatim.
**Why verbatim matters:** invented attacks run too polite, and the drill goes
soft. Real ones are blunter than anything I'd write.

### Ch 31 — The 30-Day Rollout
**Need:** one before/after number from a client floor — talk-time ratio, or
concessions-after-silence.
**Why:** the rollout reads as theory without a number that actually moved.
Even rough is fine. "Went from about seventy percent talk time to low forties
over a month" is a usable sentence.
**Extra question:** what nearly killed the rollout in week three?

## Priority 3 — the Amazon sample

### Ch 1 — The Negotiation Is Already Running
**Need:** a deal where the buyer's position was set internally before your first
contact, and finding that out changed your approach.
**Extra question:** how did you find out — who told you, and what were you

## Priority 4 — the tool chapters

One case each. Shorter is fine — 150 words. Several of these are better as
failures.

| Ch | Chapter | What's needed |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | Diagnose Before You Ask | A vague word — "complicated," "expensive," "risky" — that turned out to mean something completely different once you made them define it. |
| 5 | The Emotion Vocabulary | **A miss.** A Ready buyer who got pitched anyway, or a Torn one treated as Not Yet. What did the misread cost before you caught it? |
| 6 | Labeling | A label that surfaced an obstacle nobody had stated. |
| 7 | Mirroring | A single mirror on a loaded word — ideally where an absolute ("impossible," "final," "not interested") turned out to be conditional. |
| 8 | Tactical Silence | Holding a silence that produced the concession or the disclosure — with the count if you know it. **A case where you broke first works better.** |
| 9 | Reading the Decision Structure | A power label or mislabel that surfaced the actual decision maker — ideally where the friendly contact had no authority at all. |
| 10 | The Accusation Audit | An audit where the objection you named was exactly what they were holding. |
| 11 | The Negative Label | **The hard version:** a counterpart who was warm and evasive rather than hostile. That's the common case and nobody trains for it. |
| 12 | The Power of "No" | A no-oriented question that restarted a stalled deal — ideally where the honest answer was "yes, stop," and that turned out to be useful. |
| 13 | Validation | Validation that moved a position without conceding anything — ideally where the distrust was inherited from a previous vendor. |
| 15 | The Calibrated Question | "How am I supposed to do that?" against a hard demand, producing a concession nobody asked for. |
| 16 | The Asking Label | Intelligence a direct question wouldn't have got — competitor, budget ceiling, or hidden authority. |
| 17 | Paraphrase and Summary | A summary that isolated the last open item and closed on it — bonus if it also surfaced something unmentioned across three prior meetings. |
| 18 | Trading | A non-cash trade that closed a deal stuck on price. **Name the actual currency** — that's the whole value of the case. |
| 19 | Anchoring | A number that dissolved under probing. What was it actually based on — a different scope, a stale quote, a bad previous vendor? |
| 20 | Deadline Dynamics | A deadline that turned out to be atmosphere. What they said, what the mechanism actually was, what it changed. |

## Not yet gaps, but the biggest ones coming

**Ch 26–30, Part V.** Not in `GAP-INDEX.md` because they aren't drafted. Once
they are, they'll dominate it — five chapters at weight 4, every one needing
real client material.

This is the argument for **collecting before drafting.** If I write Part V now
it's five chapters of empty flags, and Part V is precisely where a corporate
reader can tell invented material from real. What's needed:

- **Ch 26 — The Renewal and the Price Increase.** A renewal where you raised
  price and kept the account. What did you say first?
- **Ch 27 — Procurement and the Committee You Can't See.** A deal decided by
  people you never met. How did you reach them, or how did you fail to?
- **Ch 28 — Claims, Disputes, and Escalation.** Your strongest vertical story.
  Insurance, construction, legal — whichever has the sharpest example.
- **Ch 29 — Negotiating Internally.** Up, down, or sideways inside an
  organization. Frequently the most relatable chapter in a corporate book.
- **Ch 30 — When the Protocol Fails.** **The most important case in the book.**
  A negotiation where the method did not work — bad faith, no authority, real
  deadlock — and what you did instead. A published failure is the strongest
  credibility signal available to you, and it cannot be written by anyone but
  you.

## How to send them

Whatever's easiest. Voice memo, bullet fragments, a dumped email thread with the
names taken out, or answers typed into this file under each heading. Out of
order is fine. Partial is fine — six questions answered for four chapters beats
two answered for twenty.

Anything sensitive: strip names and identifying detail before sending. The
cases work on mechanism, not on who the client was.
