# Gap Intake, answer these, don't write prose

Twenty-two chapters each need one real case. Writing twenty-two 200-word
vignettes is a miserable job and it is not the job. **Answering questions about
deals you actually ran is a different job, and it's one you can do out loud in
the car.**

So: answer the six questions below for each chapter, in fragments, badly, out
of order. I'll write the prose. What I cannot do is invent the facts, which is
why every one of these sits empty in the draft rather than being filled with
something plausible.

Ranked by `GAP-INDEX.md` priority, top of this list moves the needle most.

---

## The six questions

These turn any war story into a usable case. Same six every time.

1. **Situation.** Who, what industry, roughly what size, what was on the table?
   No names needed, "a mid-size claims operation, six-figure renewal" is
   plenty.
2. **What was said.** The actual sentence, theirs or yours, as close to verbatim
   as you remember. This is the one that matters most and the one people skip.
3. **What you expected.** What you thought was going on before the tool worked.
4. **What actually turned out to be true.** The gap between those two is the
   whole case.
5. **What changed after.** Not the outcome. The *behavior*. What did you or
   they do differently in the next ten minutes?
6. **Why it worked.** One sentence on the mechanism. If you can't name one, say
   so. That's useful too.

**What makes a case unusable:** a win with no mechanism. "And then they signed"
teaches nothing. A near-miss with a clear mechanism is worth more than a
triumph without one, and for several of these, a **failure is the better
story** (marked below).

## Priority 1. The evaluator path

These are the chapters Ch 3 tells a buyer to read before spending money. A thin
case here costs you the deal, not just the chapter.

### Ch 2. The Four Stages
**Need:** a deal that stalled because a stage got skipped, and what changed when
the sequence ran in order.
**Ideally:** the skipped stage was Disarm, that's the one most floors don't
have at all.
**Extra question:** how long was it stalled before anyone noticed the sequence
was wrong?

### Ch 33. The Manager's Scorecard
**Need:** real inter-rater data. Two managers scoring the same ten tapes is
enough.
**Why it matters more than a story:** this chapter is the audit instrument. A
rubric with no evidence that two people score it the same way is a rubric a
procurement reviewer will dismiss in one question.
**Extra question:** where did the two scorers disagree most, and did you change
the rubric because of it?

## Priority 2, installation and the one double-gap

### Ch 14. The Discipline of Not Defending · *two gaps*
**Need A:** confirm the chapter's scope is the Attack Decoder rather than a
broader survey of defending.
**Need B:** three real opening attack lines. One defensive, one from someone
who feels unheard, one purely tactical. Verbatim.
**Why verbatim matters:** invented attacks run too polite, and the drill goes
soft. Real ones are blunter than anything I'd write.

### Ch 31. The 30-Day Rollout
**Need:** one before/after number from a client floor, talk-time ratio, or
concessions-after-silence.
**Why:** the rollout reads as theory without a number that actually moved.
Even rough is fine. "Went from about seventy percent talk time to low forties
over a month" is a usable sentence.
**Extra question:** what nearly killed the rollout in week three?

## Priority 4. The tool chapters

One case each. Shorter is fine, 150 words. Several of these are better as
failures.

| Ch | Chapter | What's needed |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | Diagnose Before You Ask | A vague word, "complicated," "expensive," "risky", that turned out to mean something completely different once you made them define it. |
| 5 | The Emotion Vocabulary | **A miss.** A Ready buyer who got pitched anyway, or a Torn one treated as Not Yet. What did the misread cost before you caught it? |
| 6 | Labeling | A label that surfaced an obstacle nobody had stated. |
| 7 | Mirroring | A single mirror on a loaded word, ideally where an absolute ("impossible," "final," "not interested") turned out to be conditional. |
| 8 | Tactical Silence | Holding a silence that produced the concession or the disclosure, with the count if you know it. **A case where you broke first works better.** |
| 9 | Reading the Decision Structure | A power label or mislabel that surfaced the actual decision maker, ideally where the friendly contact had no authority at all. |
| 10 | The Accusation Audit | An audit where the objection you named was exactly what they were holding. |
| 11 | The Negative Label | **The hard version:** a counterpart who was warm and evasive rather than hostile. That's the common case and nobody trains for it. |
| 12 | The Power of "No" | A no-oriented question that restarted a stalled deal, ideally where the honest answer was "yes, stop," and that turned out to be useful. |
| 13 | Validation | Validation that moved a position without conceding anything, ideally where the distrust was inherited from a previous vendor. |
| 15 | The Calibrated Question | "How am I supposed to do that?" against a hard demand, producing a concession nobody asked for. |
| 16 | The Asking Label | Intelligence a direct question wouldn't have got, competitor, budget ceiling, or hidden authority. |
| 17 | Paraphrase and Summary | A summary that isolated the last open item and closed on it, bonus if it also surfaced something unmentioned across three prior meetings. |
| 18 | Trading | A non-cash trade that closed a deal stuck on price. **Name the actual currency**, that's the whole value of the case. |
| 19 | Anchoring | A number that dissolved under probing. What was it actually based on. A different scope, a stale quote, a bad previous vendor? |
| 20 | Deadline Dynamics | A deadline that turned out to be atmosphere. What they said, what the mechanism actually was, what it changed. |

## Part V, the five that carry the most weight

**Ch 26-30 are drafted now.** They are in `GAP-INDEX.md` and they dominate the
top of it, five chapters at weight 4, every one waiting on real client
material. An earlier version of this page said they were not written yet.
That stopped being true when the manual was finished.

Part V is where a corporate reader can tell invented material from real, so
these five are the ones to answer first among the manual's cases:

- **Ch 26. The Renewal and the Price Increase.** A renewal where you raised
  price and kept the account. What did you say first?
- **Ch 27. Procurement and the Committee You Can't See.** A deal decided by
  people you never met. How did you reach them, or how did you fail to?
- **Ch 28. Claims, Disputes, and Escalation.** Your strongest vertical story.
  Insurance, construction, legal, whichever has the sharpest example.
- **Ch 29. Negotiating Internally.** Up, down, or sideways inside an
  organization. Frequently the most relatable chapter in a corporate book.
- **Ch 30. When the Protocol Fails.** **The most important case in the book.**
  A negotiation where the method did not work, bad faith, no authority, real
  deadlock, and what you did instead. A published failure is the strongest
  credibility signal available to you, and it cannot be written by anyone but
  you.

## Added after the mandate: the new books' asks

The Drill Book and *Same Words, Bigger Rooms* added their own flagged
slots. Same six questions, same rules. These are listed cheapest first,
because most of the everyday stories are Saturday-session material you
already tell out loud.

### The everyday book, twelve stories, one per chapter

1. **Ch 1.** An argument at home or between friends where naming the
   unsaid feeling changed the temperature in under a minute.
2. **Ch 2.** A conversation where staying quiet and handing back one
   word got to the real issue after advice had failed.
3. **Ch 3.** A repair, family or client, where opening with their case
   against you reopened a door explaining could not.
4. **Ch 4.** A raise or comp conversation where naming the awkwardness
   or locating the real decision-maker changed the outcome.
5. **Ch 5.** A big purchase where probing the anchor or separating the
   currencies moved the number materially.
6. **Ch 6.** A bill, claim, or fee dispute where calm calibrated
   questions surfaced an option the first answer hid.
7. **Ch 7.** A contractor, landlord, or ongoing service story where
   scoring the relationship separately, or the snag question asked
   early, changed the engagement.
8. **Ch 8.** A job offer where slowing down, finding the band, or
   testing an exploding deadline changed the package.
9. **Ch 9.** A clean no inside a family or friendship that preserved
   what the soft yes was destroying.
10. **Ch 10.** A misread state, torn pressured, ready oversold, or not
    yet rushed, and what changed when the read was corrected.
11. **Ch 11.** One real case at real scale, six figures up, where a
    small-chapter tool visibly turned the room. This one replaces or
    anchors the chapter's composite.
12. **Ch 12.** Held to a higher bar, and only if the chapter ships at
    all: a Saturday-session moment where the belief under a position
    surfaced. Nothing composite, nothing approximate.

### The Drill Book, three asks

- **The Saturday session format as it runs now.** The Part V page is
  waiting on it and assembles nothing until it arrives.
- **Three real opening attack lines,** one per type, for the
  Three-Type Sort. Invented attacks are too polite and the drill runs
  soft.
- **Which drills have genuinely failed in a live room, and how.** The
  WHAT GOES WRONG sections are drafted from craft. Yours are from a
  thousand sessions, and they are the sections that make the book
  worth money. Bonus, and the best fix for the title: two real
  Saturday-session drills the books never captured would take the
  count from 38 to 40.

### The Part V script boxes, added Fri Aug 28

Separate from the stories, and quicker. Chapters 26 to 29 carry **40
counterpart-facing lines that were written rather than assembled**,
because the catalog has no renewal, procurement, claims or internal
family. Full accounting and three options in `book/AUTHORED-LINES.md`.

The ask is a read, not a writing job: skim the forty, mark each one
*keep*, *reword*, or *here is what I actually say*. The renewal and
claims chapters are where your own language will beat the draft by the
widest margin, and anything you reword is a candidate for the app's
catalog as well as the book.

## Six Before Yes, the guide

This book had no section on this page until now, which meant its
twenty-eight open slots were invisible unless you went looking in the
files. It is also the book with the longest lead time in the project,
because seven of its asks are other people.

### The seven practitioner voices, start these today

Each chapter carries one outside voice, 150 to 250 words, named, with
title and organization as they want it printed. They gate the
acknowledgements page and therefore the print date. Nothing else in the
project depends on somebody else's calendar, so these are the only asks
where starting late cannot be recovered by working harder later.

Seven profiles, one per chapter:

| Ch | Who to ask | What to ask them about |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Any practitioner with a name worth printing | Where negotiations are actually won |
| 2 | An agent, broker, or counsel who negotiates for clients | The negotiation before the negotiation |
| 3 | A mediator | Reading what is actually at stake. Mediators do this step for a living |
| 4 | Someone who sells or negotiates daily | Misreading a state and what it cost. An admitted misread lands harder here than mastery |
| 5 | Procurement or deal desk | Choosing strategy against your own temperament. The buyer's-side voice is what differentiates the chapter |
| 6 | Crisis, law enforcement, or hostage-adjacent | Running a sequence under real pressure |
| 7 | Aviation, medicine, military, or elite sport | Debrief culture. The outside-the-profession voice lands hardest here |

The ask is small and that is the point. One paragraph, their name on it,
in a book that is not selling anything of theirs. Most people say yes to
that and the ones who say no say it quickly.

### Fourteen cases, and three of them you are already telling

The guide's cases are shorter than the manual's, 120 to 130 words, because
the guide is a 60-page book. Three of them are the same deal a manual
chapter already asks for, told shorter. Answer the manual's version and
the guide's is a trim, not a second interview:

- **Ch 5, a deal stuck on price unstuck by a non-cash trade.** Same ask as
  manual Ch 18. Name the currency.
- **Ch 4, a deal killed or nearly killed by someone who never came to a
  meeting.** Same ask as manual Ch 27.
- **Ch 1, someone who ran the conversation well and lost outside it.**
  Close to manual Ch 1, where the position was set internally before first
  contact. It may be one story or two. Your call.

That leaves eleven genuinely new ones, all short:

| Ch | The case | Words |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The inverse. A weaker conversationalist who had done the outside work and won on it | 120 |
| 2 | A vague mandate that produced a technically successful, actually resented outcome | 120 |
| 2 | A walkaway that was nothing special but existed, and changed the outcome by existing | 120 |
| 3 | A stated demand that turned out to be a proxy, and what it was protecting | 130 |
| 3 | A shared objective named out loud that reframed a stuck negotiation | 120 |
| 5 | A negotiation won from the weak side of the table on preparation density | 130 |
| 6 | One observation named out loud that changed a conversation's temperature | 120 |
| 7 | A closed deal that came apart in implementation, and the clause or conversation that would have held it | 120 |
| 4 | A state called correctly before a meeting started, and what was run differently because of it | 120 |
| 6 | A conversation that turned sharp, and what running the stages in order recovered that a better line would not have | 120 |
| 7 | A habit the log caught across several negotiations that none of them revealed on its own | 120 |

Ch 2 and Ch 3 want two apiece. Ch 1 and Ch 5 each have one more here on
top of the shared story above. The last three were added when an audit
found Ch 4, 6 and 7 carrying one example where the blueprint's kit calls
for two. Saturday-session material works for all of them.

## How to send them

Whatever's easiest. Voice memo, bullet fragments, a dumped email thread with the
names taken out, or answers typed into this file under each heading. Out of
order is fine. Partial is fine, six questions answered for four chapters beats
two answered for twenty.

Anything sensitive: strip names and identifying detail before sending. The
cases work on mechanism, not on who the client was.
