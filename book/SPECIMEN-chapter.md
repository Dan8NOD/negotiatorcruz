# Chapter 5 — The Label

**Part I · READ**

---

## The Standard

A label is a short declarative sentence that names the other side's emotional
state out loud, offered without a question mark and without a defense. It is
the first tool in the Protocol because everything downstream depends on it:
you cannot disarm a resistance you haven't identified, and you cannot steer a
person whose actual problem you're still guessing at.

The label is not empathy as a virtue. It is empathy as a diagnostic
instrument. You say what you think they're feeling, and their reaction tells
you whether you were right. That is the entire mechanism. A label is the
cheapest, fastest test available for whether you understand the room — and
you run it before you put a number on the table, not after.

---

## When to reach for it

Use a label when:

- **You've just heard resistance and don't yet know its source.** "It's a
  budget issue" is almost never a budget issue. The label is how you find out
  what it actually is without interrogating them.
- **The temperature is rising.** Naming an emotion reliably lowers it. An
  emotion that has been said out loud stops being pressure and becomes a
  topic.
- **Someone has gone quiet.** Silence is data you can't read. A label gives
  them something to agree with or correct, which is easier than volunteering.
- **You are about to ask for something.** Label first. Always. The ask lands
  differently on a person who believes you understand their position.

Do **not** reach for it when:

- **You already know the emotion and have already labeled it accurately.**
  Labeling twice reads as technique, and the moment your counterpart notices
  technique, the tool is dead for the rest of the meeting.
- **The other side is asking a direct factual question.** "What's your
  implementation timeline?" gets an answer, not a label. Labeling a logistics
  question is the most common way a rep turns a good tool into a smug one.
- **You are the one who is upset.** A label delivered by someone managing
  their own frustration comes out as an accusation. Fix your state first, or
  use silence instead.

---

## The words

Every label points at *them*. It never starts with "I."

"I understand how you feel" puts you at the center of the sentence and asks
for credit. "It seems like this has been landing on your desk all week" puts
them at the center and asks for nothing. The first invites an argument about
whether you really do understand. The second invites information.

```
STARTERS — use these, in this order of safety

  "It seems like…"
  "It sounds like…"
  "It looks like…"
  "You seem…"          (more direct — use once trust exists)
```

```
WORKING EXAMPLES

  Pressure from their own organization
  "It seems like this timeline has put you in a rough spot with
   your own team."

  The buyer who has been burned before
  "It sounds like you've had a vendor go quiet on you after
   signing, and you're not eager to repeat it."

  Stalled with no stated reason
  "It looks like something about this has stopped feeling
   straightforward."

  A committee they can't control
  "It seems like the decision isn't really sitting with you, and
   that's frustrating in its own way."

  Price resistance, source unknown
  "It sounds like the number isn't the whole problem here."
```

Then **stop talking.** The label ends. Nothing follows it. No "…is that
right?", no "…but let me explain," no nervous addendum. A label with a
question mark stapled to the end is a request for permission, and it converts
a diagnostic into an interrogation.

---

## What good sounds like

You are listening for one of three responses, and all three are useful.

**"That's right."** The label landed. They now believe you understand their
position, which is the precondition for every other tool in this manual. Do
not celebrate it and do not immediately pivot to your ask — let it sit for a
beat, then move to Stage 2 or 3.

**A correction.** "It's not really the timeline, it's that legal hasn't seen
it yet." This is *better than being right*. You have just been handed the
actual obstacle, volunteered, by the person who was previously guarding it.
Take the correction cleanly — "Legal hasn't seen it" — and let them keep
going.

**"You're right."** Be careful with this one. "You're right" is frequently
appeasement — the sound of someone ending a conversation rather than joining
it. Treat it as a signal that the label was close enough to be polite about
and not close enough to be true. Try again, more specifically, or fall back
to silence.

The fourth outcome is no response at all. That is not failure. Give it four
seconds. Most people fill it.

---

## The drill

**"Three Emotions, Three Minutes."** Fifteen minutes, no preparation, run by
a manager with a laptop.

1. Play three minutes of a recorded call — the team's own, not a demo.
2. Everyone stays silent and writes down **three emotions** they believe the
   counterpart is experiencing.
3. Next to each, they write the **evidence**: the exact phrase, pause, or
   shift in pace that produced the read.
4. Around the table. Anyone who wrote "frustrated" with no evidence line
   doesn't get to count it.
5. Each person then converts their strongest read into a label and says it
   out loud, in full, to the room. The room's job is to catch anything that
   starts with "I" or ends with a question mark.

Run it weekly. The point is not the recording — it's the evidence column.
Reps who can't cite evidence are pattern-matching on tone, and tone is the
first thing that misleads you on a hard call.

---

## Scored on

A manager can audit all four of these from a tape without being on the call.

| Metric | Target |
|---|---|
| **Specificity** | Label names a specific state ("cornered," "exposed," "worn down"), not a generic one ("frustrated," "concerned," "upset"). Generic labels score zero. |
| **Landing rate** | Percentage of labels that draw "that's right" or a substantive correction. Below 50%, the rep is guessing rather than reading. |
| **Position** | Label was delivered *before* the first ask in the call. Binary. Yes or no. |
| **Clean delivery** | No "I," no question mark, no follow-on sentence. Binary. |

Track landing rate per rep over four weeks. It is the single most predictive
number in the Read stage, and it moves quickly once evidence-writing becomes
a habit.

---

## Failure mode

**Solving before diagnosing.** This is the most expensive habit in the stage
and it is almost always well-intentioned. The counterpart describes a
problem, the rep feels the pull to be useful, and out comes a fix — a
discount, an expedited timeline, a concession nobody asked for.

The moment you offer a solution, you have told them you were assembling a
response while they were talking. Everything you concede after that reads as
pressure rather than good faith, because it arrived before you demonstrated
you understood what you were solving.

**What it looks like on tape:** the rep's first substantive sentence contains
a number, a date, or the word "we can."

**The recovery:** stop, and label the thing you just did.

```
  "I jumped straight to fixing that, and I don't think I actually
   understood the problem yet. What's the part of this that's
   hardest for you?"
```

This works better than it has any right to. You have modeled the exact
behavior you're asking for — naming an uncomfortable truth out loud instead
of defending it — and you have handed the conversation back.

**The second failure mode, less common but harder to recover from:** the
performed label. Delivered with warmth turned up, eye contact held a half
second too long, the cadence of someone who read a book. Counterparts detect
it instantly and the correct read is that they are being handled. Labels work
because they are unremarkable. Say them flatly, the way you'd say the weather.
