## Find the Stakes

Two companies once spent three weeks deadlocked over a delivery date neither
of them actually cared about. One side's real concern was looking competent to
a new board; the other's was not being blamed if a launch slipped. The date
was just the only thing either of them could say out loud. Every negotiation
has a layer like this. Step 2 is learning to read it before you respond to
the layer above it.

*Core question: what is genuinely at stake here — and for whom?*

### §1 — Positions and stakes

A position is what someone asks for. A stake is why they need to ask. The
position is "we need it under sixty thousand." The stake might be a budget
ceiling — or a boss who said *don't come back over sixty*, or a previous
vendor who blew the budget and made the last person who approved it look
foolish. Same sentence, three different negotiations.

Here is the working rule from a thousand practice sessions: **the surface ask
is almost never the real ask, and people will tell you the real one if you
make it safe to say.** Not through interrogation — through observation said
out loud, and then silence.

The instrument is a handful of sentences that separate the layers:

> *"It seems like this is about more than the number on the page."*
> *"It sounds like the surface ask isn't the real ask."*
> *"What are you actually trying to protect in this?"*
> *"It seems like fairness matters more here than the dollars do."*
> *"Is this a won't, or is it a can't?"*

That last one deserves its own paragraph. A *won't* is a choice — it can be
traded with. A *can't* is a constraint — it can only be worked around. Most
deadlocks are one side treating a can't as a won't and pushing on a wall.
Six words sort them.

**OUR ADVICE.** Every time you hear a number, ask what it's protecting before
you decide what it's worth. Numbers are positions wearing armor.

> **METHOD — The stakes read**
>
> Before contact, build a two-line table for every party — including your
> own side, which is the half everyone skips:
>
> 1. **Their position:** what they've asked for, verbatim.
> 2. **Their stake — gain:** what they get if this lands. Include the
>    unsayable versions: looking right, a rival looking wrong, a promise kept.
> 3. **Their stake — loss:** what it costs them if it collapses. Loss
>    outweighs gain in every negotiation you will ever run; weight it
>    accordingly.
> 4. **Evidence:** the sentence, pause, or history each read is based on. No
>    evidence, no entry — an unevidenced stake is a guess wearing a suit.
> 5. **Your own row, same columns.** What are *you* protecting? If you can't
>    answer, your counterpart probably can, and now they know your stakes
>    better than you do.
>
> The table is not for the meeting. It's for the moment in the meeting when
> a demand makes no sense — because a demand that makes no sense against
> positions almost always makes perfect sense against stakes.

### §2 — The shared objective

Now the counterintuitive half of the step. Once you can see both sides'
stakes, look for the overlap — the outcome both sides genuinely need, however
small. It is nearly always there: the deal surviving implementation, neither
side's boss getting a surprise, the relationship existing next year.

Naming it is not softness. It is leverage of a specific kind: **a stated
shared objective converts you from opponent to co-author, and co-authors get
told things opponents don't.** The negotiation doesn't stop being a
negotiation — but every hard ask you make afterward is measured against the
thing you both said you wanted, instead of against your motives.

> *"Before we get into the parts we disagree on — can we agree that whatever
> we sign has to actually survive implementation?"*

One sentence like that, early, changes the geometry of everything after it.
Disagreements become engineering problems inside a shared goal rather than
attacks on each other's positions.

**FOCUS — When the shared objective is fake.** If you name a shared objective
and their behaviour never once bends toward it, you have learned something
more important than the objective: you may be in the bad-faith case, where no
stake you can serve will move them. Don't keep offering. Chapter 7's debrief
covers what to record; the companion manual's failure chapter covers what to
do.

**OUR ADVICE.** Find the shared objective before contact, but say it second —

> **DRILL — The false number and the false clock**
>
> *Scenario:* A counterpart opens with: "It's ninety thousand, and we need
> your answer by Friday." You happen to know — from Step 2 work — that both
> the number and the deadline are soft. What are they each standing in for,
> and what do you say first?
>
> *Work it before reading on.*
>
> *Worked answer:* A hard number stated first is usually protecting against
> a feared negotiation — it's armor, and armor tells you where someone
> expects to be hit. The Friday clock is almost always someone *else's*
> pressure passed through: a boss's review, a quarter closing, a colleague's
> promise. So neither gets argued with. The number gets the probe —
> *"how did you land on ninety?"* — and the clock gets the mechanism test:
> *"what actually happens on Saturday?"* Run in that order, gently, they
> separate: the ninety turns out to be last year's spend plus insurance, and
> Friday turns out to be a status meeting, not a wall. You have not
> countered, conceded, or challenged anyone — and both pieces of armor are
> now on the table instead of on them.

> **KEY POINTS TO REMEMBER**
>
> **§1 Positions and stakes**
> - A position is the ask. A stake is why they need to ask. Respond to the
>   second.
> - "Is this a won't, or a can't?" — a won't can be traded with; a can't can
>   only be worked around.
> - Build the stakes table before contact, with evidence, including your own
>   row.
>
> **§2 The shared objective**
> - Naming the overlap converts opponent into co-author — and co-authors get
>   told things.
> - Find it before contact; say it after listening.
> - If behaviour never bends toward the stated shared objective, you're not
>   in a stakes problem. You're in a faith problem.
