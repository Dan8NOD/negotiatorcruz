## Pick Your Path

Ask a negotiator what their strategy is and you'll usually hear a
description of their personality. The collaborative person plans to
collaborate, the competitive person plans to compete, and both call it
strategy. Step 4 is the discipline of choosing your approach from the
situation in front of you — which occasionally means choosing one you don't
enjoy.

---

> **IN THIS CHAPTER**
> §1 — Reading the balance of power, honestly — including when you're weaker

---

### §1 — The balance of power

Strategy starts with an honest audit of leverage — yours and theirs — and
"honest" is the operative word, because negotiators systematically flatter
their own position and inflate the other side's.

Leverage isn't one thing. It's three, and most people only ever hunt the
first:

**Positive leverage** — you have something they want. The obvious kind.
**Negative leverage** — you can cost them something they fear losing. Usually
the stronger kind, because people work harder to avoid a loss than to chase a
gain — and usually the kind best *never said out loud*: a switching cost they
compute themselves is a fact, while the same cost in your mouth is a threat.
**Normative leverage** — their own stated standards, held against an
inconsistency in their position. The quietest kind, the most underused, and
the safest: *"you told us predictability mattered more than price"* is an
argument they have to resolve, not one they can resent.

Audit all three, both directions. Then place yourself on the honest grid:
when you're strong, you can set anchors and pace; when you're weak, your
strategy is preparation density — knowing their stakes, their room, and their
clock better than they know yours. Weak-position negotiation is won on
homework, and it's won surprisingly often, because strong parties get lazy
in direct proportion to their strength.

**OUR ADVICE.** Write down which of the three leverages you actually hold,
with evidence. If the honest answer is "positive only, and mild," you don't
have a strategy problem — you have a Step 1 problem. Go improve your exit
before you engage.

### §2 — The path

With the balance read, three decisions make the plan. This is the shortest
METHOD box in the book, and the one most often skipped entirely.

> **METHOD — The path, in three decisions**
>
> 1. **Choose the strategy from the quadrant, not from your comfort.**
>    Compete where you're strong and it's one-shot. Collaborate where the
>    relationship outlasts the deal. Trade hardest where interests differ
>    most — difference, not similarity, is where value comes from. And if
>    every quadrant looks bad: don't engage yet. Changing the conditions is
>    also a strategy.
> 2. **Build the trade set before the room.** Two columns — what we can
>    give, what they can give — *money allowed only once per column.* Score
>    each item: cost to giver, value to receiver. Cheap-to-give,
>    valuable-to-receive is your currency. A concession is never given;
>    it is swapped, and the swap is named in the same breath.
> 3. **Set the stop, in writing, before contact.** The number — from Step
>    1's mandate — where you walk. Written down because the room will move
>    you: deadlines, sunk time, and a likable counterpart all argue for one
>    more concession, and a stop that lives in your head bends. Paper
>    doesn't.

**FOCUS — The plan is not a script.** A path is a direction plus a trade set
plus a stop. It is not a sequence of things you will say — the room will
destroy any script inside ten minutes, and negotiators clutching a dead
script negotiate worse than ones with no plan at all, because they experience
every surprise as a failure. Plan the *decisions*, improvise the *sentences*.
The sentences are Chapter 6's job.

> **DRILL — Two defensible paths**
>
> *Scenario:* A renewal, mildly in your favor. Path A: open strong on price,
> compete, take the margin — the relationship can absorb it. Path B: hold
> price flat, trade the increase for a two-year term and a reference — the
> margin can wait. Both are defensible. Pick one, then work out what the
> other would have cost you.
>
> *Work it before reading on.*
>
> *Worked answer:* The choice isn't found in the numbers — both paths price
> out acceptably. It's found in Step 2 and Step 3 work: *what state is the
> decider in, and what outlasts this deal?* A Ready decider with a healthy
> relationship absorbs Path A cleanly — they want done, and the margin is
> real. But the same Path A run at a Torn decider hands them a fresh reason
> to deliberate, stalls the renewal into next quarter, and spends
> relationship you'll want at the *next* renewal — which is the cost almost
> nobody prices: Path A's margin arrives once; Path B's term and reference
> compound. The drill's real lesson is that "which path?" is rarely a
> pricing question. It's a question about time horizon and state, decided
> before the room — which is why strategy is Step 4 and not Step 5.

> **KEY POINTS TO REMEMBER**
>
> **§1 The balance of power**
> - Three leverages: positive, negative, normative. Audit all three, both
>   directions, with evidence.
> - Negative leverage is strongest said least — let them compute the loss.
> - Weak positions are won on preparation density. Strong parties get lazy.
>
> **§2 The path**
> - Choose strategy from the quadrant, not from your personality.
> - Build the trade set before the room; money appears once per column.
> - Write the stop down and tell a witness. A stop in your head bends.
> - Plan the decisions, improvise the sentences.
