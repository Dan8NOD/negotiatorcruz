## Know Your Exit

The client's brief was eleven words: "just get us the best deal you can, don't
lose them." Six weeks later the deal closed on terms the client hated, and the
negotiator who delivered exactly what was asked for was somehow the one
explaining themselves. Nothing in the room caused that. It was lost in the
eleven words.

### §1 — Authority and its limits

Every negotiation you run on someone else's behalf — a client's, your
company's, your family's — begins with a mandate, and almost every mandate you
will ever receive is defective. "Get the best deal you can." "See what they'll
do on price." "Don't come back without something." These are not mandates.
They are moods, and a negotiation executed against a mood ends with the
negotiator blamed for a decision nobody ever actually made.

A real mandate answers four things: the objective (what does *good* look like,
in numbers), the floor (where do we stop), the trade space (what am I allowed
to give), and the escalation line (which decisions are mine, and which go
back). Extracting those from the person who briefed you is itself a
negotiation — usually your first one of the deal, and the one most people
skip because it feels like admitting you can't be trusted with ambiguity.

It is the opposite. The question "what would make you regret sending me?"
is not weakness; it is the sound of someone who intends to be accountable for
the right thing.

**OUR ADVICE.** Never accept a mandate you couldn't repeat back as four
numbers and a sentence. If you can't, the negotiation hasn't started — the
briefing is still going.

**EXAMPLE — The eleven-word brief.** `[NEEDS: real case — a vague mandate that
produced a technically-successful, actually-resented outcome. 120 words.]`

> **METHOD — Extracting a mandate from a mood**
>
> Run these four questions, in order, against any vague brief:
>
> 1. **"If this goes perfectly, what number am I coming back with?"**
>    Forces the objective out of adjectives and into arithmetic.
> 2. **"What's the number where you'd rather I walked?"**
>    The floor. If they won't name one, you don't have a mandate — you have
>    a hope with your name attached.
> 3. **"What am I allowed to give to get that?"**
>    The trade space: term, timing, scope, exclusivity. Listed, not implied.
> 4. **"Which calls are mine in the room, and which do I bring back?"**
>    The escalation line — decided now, calmly, instead of mid-deal by
>    whoever feels strongest that day.
>
> Write the four answers down and send them back to whoever briefed you, in
> one paragraph, before first contact. That paragraph is the mandate. The
> reply — even a one-word "yes" — is what protects you when the deal gets
> uncomfortable, which it will.

### §2 — The exit

Now the second half of the step, and the more famous one: your walkaway.

The person who needs the deal less controls it. Everyone has heard some
version of that sentence; almost nobody acts on it, because acting on it
requires something uncomfortable — a real, specific, survivable answer to
*"what do I do if this falls through?"* Not a brave answer. A true one.

Here is what most people miss: **your alternative doesn't have to be good.
It has to exist, and you have to have looked at it.** The negotiator who has
genuinely priced their fallback — even a mediocre one — walks in without
desperation, and desperation is the one leak no technique in Chapter 6 can
patch. Counterparts smell it the way dogs smell fear, and every number you
say afterward is discounted by it.

The same math runs in reverse. Before contact, ask: *what happens to them if
this falls through?* A landlord with three empty units has a weaker exit than
one with a waitlist. A vendor at quarter-end has a weaker exit than the same
vendor in week two. You do not need to say any of this out loud — knowing it
changes what you'll accept, which changes what you'll get.

**FOCUS — The exit you're not allowed to fake.** Bluffing a walkaway you
don't have works until it's called, and it gets called precisely when the
stakes are highest — because that's when the other side checks. If your true
position is "I cannot walk," the honest play is to *change that fact before
negotiating* — build one alternative, even a poor one — rather than perform a
strength you'll have to fold on. A folded bluff doesn't return you to
neutral. It reprices everything you say afterward.

**EXAMPLE — The mediocre alternative that won.** `[NEEDS: real case — a
walkaway that was nothing special but existed, and changed the outcome by
existing. 120 words.]`

**OUR ADVICE.** Before first contact, write one sentence beginning "If this
falls through, I will…" and make it true. If you can't finish the sentence,
finish it before you dial — that hour is worth more than any hour of script
practice.

**EXPERT VOICE.** `[NEEDS: named practitioner, 150–250 words, on mandates —
ideally someone who negotiates on behalf of clients: agent, broker, counsel.
Theme: "the negotiation before the negotiation."]`

> **DRILL — The eleven-word brief**
>
> *Scenario:* A client briefs you exactly as this chapter opened: "just get
> us the best deal you can, don't lose them." You have ten minutes with them
> before they board a flight. What do you ask, and in what order?
>
> *Work it before reading on.*
>
> *Worked answer:* The order matters more than the wording. **Objective
> first** — "if this goes perfectly, what number am I coming back with?" —
> because every later answer calibrates against it. **Floor second**, phrased
> as their regret, not yours: "what's the number where you'd rather I
> walked?" The regret framing gets a real answer where "what's my limit"
> gets "use your judgment." **Trade space third** — "if they won't move on
> price, what am I allowed to give instead?" — which converts "don't lose
> them" from an anxiety into a resource list. **Escalation last**, and in
> writing from the gate: "anything outside those numbers, I pause and call
> you — agreed?" Ten minutes, four answers, and the difference between
> negotiating on a mandate and auditioning for blame.

> **KEY POINTS TO REMEMBER**
>
> **§1 Authority and its limits**
> - Most mandates are moods. A real one is four numbers and a sentence.
> - Extracting the mandate is your first negotiation — with your own side.
> - Confirm it in writing before contact. The reply is your protection.
>
> **§2 The exit**
> - The person who needs the deal less controls it — and need is a fact you
>   can change before you negotiate, not just a feeling to manage.
> - Your alternative doesn't have to be good. It has to exist and be priced.
> - Never bluff an exit. A called bluff reprices everything after it.
