## Lock and Log

The handshake felt like the finish line. Five weeks later the "agreement" is

### §1 — Locking

A yes is not an agreement. A yes is a mood, and moods expire. What converts
one into the other is specificity about implementation — who does what, by
when, and what happens at the first snag — agreed *while everyone is still
in the room*, because the room is the last place this will ever be easy.

Three locks, in order:

**Test the yes.** One agreement is a data point; the same agreement
confirmed three ways is a pattern you can trust. Get it as a plain
commitment. Get it summarized back until they say "that's right." Then get
it as an answer to a how-question — *"how do we make sure the first invoice
doesn't surprise anyone?"* If the story wobbles on the third pass, you've
found the real objection now, while it's cheap, instead of in week five.

**Sweep the residue.** Before anything is signed: *"what did I miss that
would make this better?"* — or the sharper version, *"what's the thing you
almost brought up but didn't?"* There is always one. Invited now, it's a
small ask you can grant or trade; suppressed, it becomes the first support
escalation, arriving with no goodwill attached.

**Schedule the snag.** *"How do you want to handle it if we hit a problem
halfway through?"* asked before signature does two jobs: it produces an
actual mechanism — a named contact, a response time — and it quietly
presupposes there *is* a halfway through. Deals with a snag plan survive
their first problem. Deals without one convert their first problem into a
renegotiation.

**OUR ADVICE.** Never leave the room with only a yes. Leave with a date, a
name, and a next step — or you left with a mood.

### §2 — Logging

Here is the argument of the whole book in one uncomfortable question: after
your last significant negotiation, what did you write down?

For almost everyone the honest answer is nothing — which means every lesson
it contained is now stored in memory, and memory edits negotiations in your
favor with remarkable consistency. The wins get cleaner, the concessions get
more necessary, and the moment you broke first gets shorter every time you
retell it. Experience without a record isn't ten years of learning. It's one
year, repeated ten times, with improving reviews.

The fix costs three sentences.

> **METHOD — The three-sentence log**
>
> Within a day of any significant negotiation — sooner is better, memory
> starts editing immediately:
>
> 1. **What I did.** The actual moves, including the ones that embarrass
>    you. *"Held the price. Broke the silence at four seconds. Conceded
>    the fee unprompted."*
> 2. **What they did.** Their moves, their state, what shifted them —
>    evidence, not interpretation.
> 3. **What I'll do differently next time.** One thing. Specific enough
>    to actually do: not "be more patient" but "count six seconds before
>    answering any number."
>
> That's the whole instrument. Over thirty days the log becomes a mirror —
> your patterns, visible: where you rush, where you over-explain, where you
> break. Over a year it becomes the thing this chapter's figure shows: the
> last step of every negotiation feeding the first step of the next,
> because sentence three of today's log is Step 1 homework for the next
> deal. That loop is the capability. Everything else is technique.

*[Figure 7 — the loop closed: Step 6 arcing back to Step 1, the six-node

**FOCUS — Log the wins too.** The instinct is to debrief only the losses.
Wrong twice: wins contain your best moves, currently invisible to you
because they felt natural — and un-debriefed wins breed the most expensive
belief in negotiation, which is that you win because of who you are rather
than because of what you did. What you did can be repeated on a bad day.
Who you are goes missing on one.

**OUR ADVICE.** Put the three sentences somewhere they accumulate — one
notebook, one file, one thread. Thirty scattered debriefs are thirty
notes; thirty in one place are a pattern, and the pattern is the point.

> **DRILL — The clause that breaks in a month**
>
> *Scenario:* A services agreement, signed Friday, both sides genuinely
> pleased. Payment: "50% on signature, 50% on completion." Delivery: "final
> assets within 60 days." Also agreed, verbally, with warmth: "and of
> course we can iterate until you're happy." Something here breaks inside a
> month. Find the clause — and the conversation that should have happened.
>
> *Work it before reading on.*
>
> *Worked answer:* The break is the collision between "50% on completion"
> and "iterate until you're happy" — because *completion* now has two
> owners and no definition. Day 60 arrives, the assets are delivered, the
> client wants one more round, and both sides are right: the vendor has
> completed, the client isn't happy. The second invoice ages while goodwill
> curdles into position-taking — over a clause both sides remember
> agreeing to. The conversation that should have happened is Step 6's
> snag-scheduling, run before signature: *"how do we make sure 'done'
> means the same thing to both of us — how many rounds, and what happens
> if we want more?"* Two minutes, mildly awkward, and it converts the
> book's thesis into a paycheck: the lock isn't paperwork after the win.
> It's the last negotiation of the deal, and skipping it un-wins the room
> work retroactively.

> **KEY POINTS TO REMEMBER**
>
> **§1 Locking**
> - A yes is a mood. A date, a name, and a next step is an agreement.
> - Test the yes three ways; sweep for the thing they almost brought up;
>   schedule the snag before signature.
> - "Done" needs one definition with two owners' signatures on it.
>
> **§2 Logging**
> - Memory edits in your favor. Three sentences within a day beats total
>   recall next quarter.
> - Sentence three of today's log is Step 1 of the next deal — that loop
>   is the capability.
> - Debrief the wins. What you did can be repeated on a bad day; who you
>   are cannot.
