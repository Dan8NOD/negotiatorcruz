## Map the Room

The friendliest person in the deal is rarely the most important one. The most
important one is often somebody you haven't met, whose name hasn't come up,
and who will spend ninety seconds on your proposal in a meeting you'll never
see. Step 3 is finding that person before they find your proposal.

*Core question: who decides, who influences, who can quietly kill this — and

---

### §1 — Mapping the other side

The other side is not a person. It is an organization wearing a person, and
the person you're talking to occupies exactly one seat in it. Before any
consequential negotiation, you need a read on four seats:

**The decider** — can say yes and make it stick. **The influencer** — can't
say yes, but the decider asks them first. **The blocker** — can't say yes
either, but can make yes impossible, and often earned that power by being
burned before. **The messenger** — the person actually in front of you, who
may be any of the above or none of them.

You will rarely be told which seat you're talking to, and asking outright —
*"are you the decision maker?"* — is faintly insulting and reliably answered
with a lie. The room tells you anyway, if you listen for pronouns:

> **"I"** — *"I'd want to see the security review"* — real ownership.
> **"We"** — *"we'd need to think about it"* — a committee exists. Find it.
> **"They"** — *"they'll never approve that"* — authority is elsewhere, and
> your contact has already positioned themselves as a bystander.
> **A name** — *"Rachel will ask about terms"* — a gift. Write it down.
> **A switch mid-sentence** — *"I think we could— well, they'd have to sign
> off"* — you just watched their real authority narrow in front of you.

When the pronouns won't settle it, one flat observation usually will:
*"It seems like this needs a yes from someone else in the room first."* Said
warmly, it is almost impossible to leave uncorrected — confirmation and
denial are both information, and both beat guessing.

**OUR ADVICE.** Count the people with an opinion about your deal, then count
the ones you can name. If the first number is bigger — it is — the gap is
your risk, and it's where the next conversation should aim.

> **METHOD — The room map**
>
> One page per consequential negotiation, before contact where possible:
>
> 1. **Draw the seats.** Decider, influencer(s), blocker(s), messenger —
>    named where you can, marked "unknown" where you can't. The unknowns are
>    the work list.
> 2. **Mark the evidence.** Told directly / inferred / assumed. Three colors.
>    Most first-draft maps are two facts and six assumptions dressed alike.
> 3. **Trace the yes.** Draw the actual path a signature takes. Every hop is
>    a place the deal can stall; every hop you can't draw is a place it
>    already has.
> 4. **State each seat.** Torn, Ready, or Not Yet — §2's grid. The decider's
>    state matters most; the blocker's is the one most often misread.
> 5. **One question per unknown.** Written verbatim, deployed on the next
>    call. The map is never finished — it's a standing list of what to ask.

### §2 — The state grid

*[The parallel grid runs here as the centerfold — one page per state,
identical ten fields, from `02-parallel-grid.md`. The spread readers
photograph.]*

Everything in §1 tells you *where* someone sits. The grid tells you *when
they are* — because the same decider is a different negotiation on Tuesday
than on Thursday.

Most typologies sort people into permanent types. This book doesn't, on
principle: people are not Analysts or Assertives all day. They move between
three **states** — Torn, Ready, Not Yet — sometimes inside a single meeting,
and the state in front of you right now is the only thing you can negotiate
with.

The reads are behavioural, not psychic. **Torn** repeats both sides of the
argument unprompted and asks for information they don't use. **Ready** asks
about start dates instead of features and corrects your caution. **Not Yet**
is warm, agreeable, reschedules easily, and nothing you say changes their
week. Each state has a tool set, a repair move, and — most usefully — a worst
move, printed in the grid. The worst moves are worth memorizing before the
tools: adding options to a Torn, re-arguing a case to a Ready, and
manufacturing urgency at a Not Yet are the three most common self-inflicted
wounds in everyday negotiation.

**FOCUS — Your own column.** You took the State Check on page four (if you
didn't, now — the two minutes pay for themselves here). Your default state
under pressure is the column you will systematically misread in others,
because it doesn't look like a state from the inside; it looks like being
right. Torn readers see careful deliberation where a counterpart sees
stalling. Ready readers see healthy momentum where a counterpart feels
steamrolled. Read your own column as a counterpart would. That exercise is
worth the price of the book, and it's why the assessment comes before this
chapter.

**OUR ADVICE.** Say the state call out loud — literally, before your next
sentence, silently in company: *"Torn."* Diagnosis before speech, every
exchange. The habit takes two weeks and never leaves.

> **DRILL — The messenger with no mandate
>
> *Scenario:* Three meetings in, everything is warm, and nothing has moved.
> The person across the table agrees with everything and commits to nothing.
> You begin to suspect they have no authority — and won't say so. How do you
> find out without humiliating them, and what changes once you know?
>
> *Work it before reading on.*
>
> *Worked answer:* The pronouns have probably already told you — three
> meetings of "we" and "they" with no "I" is the fingerprint. Confirm with
> one warm observation: *"It seems like this needs a yes from someone else
> in the room first."* Either they confirm — relief, usually, because
> pretending to authority is exhausting — or they claim the authority, which
> makes it socially expensive to hide behind a committee later. Both
> outcomes beat the third option, which is four more warm meetings. Once
> confirmed, the job changes completely: stop selling to the messenger and
> start **arming** them — the one-page version, the answers to the questions
> the real decider will ask, the case that survives being made by somebody
> other than you. And protect the relationship while you widen it: *"you've
> been carrying this internally — who else should be in the next
> conversation so you're not the only one defending it?"* The messenger who
> was a bottleneck becomes a coalition partner, and the map gets its
> missing name.
