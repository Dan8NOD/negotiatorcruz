## Page 3: Introduction

> Five things I believe about negotiation, stated flatly so you can decide
> early whether to keep reading.
>
> **One. The room is about one sixth of the work.** Most of what decides a
> negotiation happens before anyone sits down, and almost none of it looks
> like negotiating. This is the whole argument of the book and the reason the
> steps are weighted the way they are.
>
> **Two. You cannot learn this from a book, including this one.** Negotiation
> is a physical skill. It lives in what you do in the four seconds after you
> ask a question, not in what you know about the question. Everything here has
> a drill attached for that reason.
>
> **Three. Technique without leverage is theater.** These tools change what
> happens with the leverage in the room. They do not manufacture alternatives,
> budget or time. A prepared negotiator with no walkaway still loses to an
> unprepared one who has somewhere else to go.
>
> **Four. People are not types. They are in states.** Most systems sort your
> counterpart into a permanent category. This one reads what is in front of
> you right now, because it changes while you watch, and producing that change
> is the job.
>
> **Five. How you win decides what you get next.** A negotiation that leaves
> the other side feeling handled produces a signed contract and a counterpart
> who spends the next year looking for the exit. Every renewal is a bill for
> how the first conversation was run.
>
> If you disagree with the first one, this book will annoy you for sixty
> pages. If you agree with it and have never acted on it, that gap is exactly
> what the six steps are for.

## Page 4: How to use this guide

> **Read it in order, once.** The steps are sequential. Step 3 assumes Step 2
> happened, and jumping to the techniques chapter is the single most common way
> to get nothing out of a method like this.
>
> **Then use it out of order, forever.** Each chapter carries a METHOD box
> with the procedure and a DRILL with a worked answer. Before a real
> negotiation, open the step you are weakest on and read those two pages.
>
> **Before you go further, take the State Check.**
>
> Two minutes, twelve questions, at **negotiatorsondemand.com**. It returns
> your default state under pressure: Torn, Ready, or Not Yet.
>
> You will need that result at Chapter 4, where the state grid runs. Your own
> column is the one you will systematically misread in other people, because
> from the inside it does not look like a state. It looks like being right.
> Reading your own column as a counterpart would is the most uncomfortable
> two pages in this guide and the most useful.
>
> **Marked terms.** Words with an asterisk\* are defined in the glossary at
> the back. Each one is marked the first time it appears in a chapter, not
> every time.
>
> **The companion volume.** Step 5 is one chapter here. It is thirty-three
> chapters in *The Cruz Protocol*, which is the operator's manual for what
> happens inside the conversation. If Step 5 is the step you keep losing,
> that is the book to buy next. If Steps 1 through 4 are where you lose, stay
> here. Most people lose there and assume they lost in the room.

## Page 5: The standard

> The tools in this guide work on people who trust you.
>
> That fact is why the standard exists rather than a reason to avoid saying
> it out loud:
>
> **These tools are used to create clarity, not confusion. More dignity, not
> less. To listen to understand, not to win. When the conversation ends, the
> other side should not feel beaten. They should feel heard.**
>
> This is not softness. It is the commercial argument, and Step 6 is where it
> gets paid: the negotiation you run this quarter sets the price of the one
> you run next year with the same person.
>
> Any technique that requires the other side not to notice is outside this
> standard. If you find one in here, it is an error and I want to know.
