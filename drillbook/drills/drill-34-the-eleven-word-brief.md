# Drill 34: The Eleven-Word Brief

*Trains: converting a vague mandate into objective, floor, trade
space, and escalation, in ten minutes.*

```
TIME    15 min          PEOPLE    2 per pair
NEEDS   nothing
STAGE   Before contact  PART      Pairs
SOURCE  Six Before Yes, Step 1
```

## Open it

> "Fifteen minutes, pairs. Your client hands you eleven words, 'just
> get us the best deal you can, don't lose them,' and boards a
> flight in ten minutes. Negotiators, you get four answers out of
> them before the door closes. The order is the drill. Go."

## Run it

1. One person plays the client with a real deal in mind, holding
   the eleven-word brief and answering only what is actually asked.
   One person plays the negotiator with ten minutes on the clock.
2. The negotiator works the four answers out, **in this order.**
   Objective first: "If this goes perfectly, what number am I
   coming back with?" Floor second, phrased as the client's regret:
   "What's the number where you'd rather I walked?" Trade space
   third: "If they won't move on price, what am I allowed to give
   instead?" Escalation last, and in writing before the gate:
   "Anything outside those numbers, I pause and call you, agreed?"
3. The client answers the way real clients do: the floor question
   gets "use your judgment" the first time, and the negotiator has
   to re-ask it through the regret framing to get a number.
4. Stop at ten minutes regardless. Score what got captured.
5. Swap, new deal, run it again.

## Score it

Four boxes: objective as a number, floor as a number, trade space
as a list, escalation agreed out loud. Then the order check from
the tape or the observer: a negotiator who asks the floor before
the objective gets numbers with no calibration, and a negotiator
who skips the writing on the escalation has a mandate that will
dissolve at the first surprise. The pass is four boxes, in order,
inside ten minutes.

## What goes wrong

**"Use your judgment" gets accepted.** It feels like trust and it
is actually blame deferred. The regret framing exists because it
converts a compliment into a number, and the drill fails softly
every time a negotiator banks the compliment instead.

**The negotiator asks all four as a checklist.** Fired off in
ninety seconds, no follow-up, boxes ticked, mandate hollow. Each
answer earns one clarifying push before the next question. Ten
minutes is enough for exactly that and nothing else, which is why
the clock is part of the drill.

**The client invents a fantasy deal.** Real deals only, same rule
as every drill in this book. An invented mandate has no real
floor, and the negotiator can feel the difference even when they
cannot name it.

## Harder

The client boards early. At minute six, they stand up. The
negotiator must choose which unasked question dies, and defend the
choice in the debrief. There is a right answer, the escalation
agreement, because it is the one that recovers the others later.

## Debrief

One question: "Which answer would you not have gotten without the
exact phrasing?" It is nearly always the floor, and the room
learns that the wording was not politeness. It was the tool.
