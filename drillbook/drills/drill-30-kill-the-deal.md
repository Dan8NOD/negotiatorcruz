# Drill 30: Kill the Deal

*Trains: diagnosing the deals the Protocol cannot save, and saying so
out loud.*

```
TIME    25 min          PEOPLE    3 to 8, quarterly
NEEDS   live pipeline, each rep's oldest open deal
STAGE   Read            PART      Live fire
SOURCE  The Cruz Protocol, Ch 30
```

## Open it

> "Twenty-five minutes, and we run this once a quarter. Bring your
> oldest open deal. We're going to test it for the three conditions
> no tool fixes, bad faith, no authority, genuine deadlock, and at
> the end each of us names one deal we're killing this week. Out
> loud, with a sentence attached. Go."

## Run it

1. Each rep brings their **oldest open deal.**
2. They run all three diagnoses out loud, with **evidence, not
   impressions**: bad faith (no intention of agreeing), no authority
   (cannot agree), genuine deadlock (both right, incompatible).
   "They reschedule but keep asking for pricing" is evidence.
   "They seem shady" is not.
3. Then Chapter 30's four questions, answered honestly in front of
   the room: what happens if I negotiate, what happens if I don't,
   does this violate anything I hold, and who else is affected.
4. The hard part: **each rep names one deal they will kill this
   week**, and drafts the acknowledgement sentence they will use,
   verbatim from the shipped family: "Sounds like that's a no."
   "It sounds like this conversation has run its course for you."
   "It looks like there's real pressure on you to end this one way
   or another."
5. Next session **opens** with what happened, before anything else.

## Score it

Deals killed versus deals named, and the reopen rate. A third of
clean acknowledgements get answered with "no, wait," and the
negotiation reopens on honest terms. Both numbers argue for the
drill: the kills return forecast honesty, the reopens return real
deals. Track the pipeline's average deal age quarterly. It drops.

## What goes wrong

**Rooms resist step four, which is the point.** Most pipelines carry
deals everyone privately knows are dead and nobody will say so,
because a dead deal removed from the forecast is a visible loss and
a dead deal left in it is an invisible one. The manager goes first,
killing one of their own, or the room will produce nothing but
diagnoses.

**The diagnosis becomes an alibi.** "Bad faith" gets stamped on any
deal that is merely hard, because it exits the drill with dignity.
The evidence rule is the guard: each diagnosis needs a stated
behavior, and the room audits it like the boxes in Map the Room.

**The kill happens in silence.** A deal quietly removed from the
CRM without the acknowledgement sentence teaches the rep nothing
and leaves the counterpart a loose end. The sentence is the drill's
second half. It gets said, to the actual person, this week.

## Harder

Run the three diagnoses on a deal the rep believes is healthy. A
healthy deal passes in two minutes and the room learns what passing
sounds like. A "healthy" deal that stalls under the same questions
was already dead, and better to hear it here than at quarter end.

## Debrief

One question: "What kept the deal you just killed alive this long?"
The answers are never about the buyer. They are about the forecast,
the manager, or the hope, and the room hearing that is what changes
next quarter's pipeline.
