# Drill 29: No Exit

*Trains: negotiating with someone you have to work with next week,
without authority or threats.*

```
TIME    20 min          PEOPLE    2 per pair
NEEDS   one live internal negotiation per person
STAGE   Steer           PART      Pairs
SOURCE  The Cruz Protocol, Ch 29
```

## Open it

> "Twenty minutes, pairs, real internal situations. The constraint
> is the drill: no escalating, no invoking anyone's title, no
> deadline threats. And remember, you work with this person again
> next week, and they will remember how this went. Go."

## Run it

1. Each person brings a **live internal negotiation.** A request they
   need to make, headcount, a deadline change, another team's
   priorities, or a demand they are currently absorbing.
2. Run it with one hard constraint: **the operator may not escalate,
   may not invoke authority, and may not threaten a deadline.** All
   three exits are closed. What remains is the toolkit: labels,
   calibrated questions, trades. "What's making this harder than it
   needs to be?" "If I move on this, what moves on your side?"
3. Second constraint, stated out loud before the round: **the
   operator has to work with this person again next week**, and the
   counterpart is instructed to remember how it went.
4. Score the outcome and the relationship **separately.** Both,
   every time, on the board as two columns. The habit of scoring
   them separately is the chapter's whole argument in two columns.
5. Then the reversal: run the same scenario once more with
   escalation permitted, and afterwards the counterpart says, out
   loud, how they would behave in the next negotiation with this
   person.

## Score it

Two columns per round: outcome, and what the counterpart says the
relationship is now worth. Step five makes the argument better than
any explanation. The escalated version usually wins the scenario
and visibly damages the next one, and the room hears the damage
priced by the person who will collect it.

## What goes wrong

**Authority leaks in politely.** "Leadership is pretty focused on
this" is an escalation wearing a lanyard, same as the title trick
in Map the Room. The counterpart calls it, the round restarts. The
drill only works with all three exits genuinely closed.

**The scenarios come in pre-softened.** People bring internal asks
they have already half-won. Require the one that is actually stuck.
The stuck ones are stuck exactly because escalation felt like
the only move, which is the belief this drill exists to break.

**The relationship score gets inflated.** Counterparts grade kindly
because the operator is sitting right there. The instruction: score
how you would actually behave next time, not how you feel now. The
reversal round usually recalibrates this on its own.

## Harder

The counterpart holds a genuine grievance about a previous
interaction, stated at the start. Negotiating the current ask
across an unrepaired past, without escalating, is the version
managers face weekly, and it chains this drill to The Repair's
toolkit.

## Debrief

One question: "Which closed exit did you reach for first?" Nearly
everyone has a favorite, authority, escalation, or the clock, and
naming it tells each person exactly which reflex their next real
internal negotiation will have to survive.
