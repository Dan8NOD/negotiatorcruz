# Drill 33: Score Twelve Rows

*Trains: auditing a real call against the scorecard, in public, with
no half points.*

```
TIME    15 min          PEOPLE    3 to 8, Fridays
NEEDS   two recorded calls, the Appendix B scorecard, copies for all
STAGE   All four        PART      The room
SOURCE  The Cruz Protocol, Ch 33
```

## Open it

> "Fifteen minutes. Two calls, twelve rows each, zero or one per
> row, no half points. A half point is a judgment about intent, and
> intent is not auditable. First call scored is mine. Go."

## Run it

1. Play the first call. Everyone scores the twelve rows from the
   manager's scorecard silently as it runs: the five Read rows
   (talk time, label before any number, silence after the label,
   corrections taken cleanly, decision structure surfaced), the two
   Disarm rows (objection named first, no defending), the three
   Steer rows (calibrated question before the first number, no
   unrequested concession, a summary that drew agreement), and the
   two Close rows (silence held after the final number, a next
   action with an owner and a date).
2. **Zero or one per row.** The no-half-points rule is the drill.
   Every argument for a half point is an argument about what the
   rep meant, and the tape does not record intent.
3. Compare scores row by row. Disagreements get thirty seconds of
   replay, not thirty seconds of debate. The tape settles it or
   the row scores zero.
4. In week one, the first call scored belongs to the manager or a
   volunteer. Never open this drill on the weakest rep's tape. You
   will not get another volunteer for a month.
5. Score the second call faster. The speed is part of the training.
   A manager who needs an hour per call will audit four calls a
   quarter and coach from vibes the rest of the time.

## Score it

The drill scores itself twice over: the calls get their totals, and
the room gets a convergence number, how many rows the scorers
agreed on out of twelve. Track convergence weekly. When it passes
ten, the floor has a shared standard, and the scorecard has become
what Chapter 33 built it to be: an instrument instead of an
opinion.

## What goes wrong

**Rows get relitigated as unfair.** "Row 9 is impossible on a
renewal." Maybe, and the row still scores zero or one today. Rule
changes happen between sessions, in writing, or the instrument
dissolves into exceptions.

**The total becomes the headline.** A 6 outscoring a 4 matters
less than which rows went to zero, and the coaching (Drill 32)
comes from the rows. Read totals last, rows first.

**Scoring drifts into commentary.** "And honestly the discovery was
weak too." Not a row. The scorecard's power is what it refuses to
measure. Park everything else.

## Harder

Hand the scorecard to the rep whose call it is, and have them score
themselves alongside the room. The self-score converging with the
room's score is the exact moment a rep stops needing the manager
in the loop, which is what the whole install is for.

## Debrief

One question: "Which row went to zero on both calls?" That row is
next Monday's fifteen minutes, chosen by evidence instead of
instinct.
