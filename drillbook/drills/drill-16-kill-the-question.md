# Drill 16: Kill the Question

*Trains: converting your most wanted questions into asking labels, after
seeing what asking them directly would reveal.*

```
TIME    15 min          PEOPLE    1 solo, or 3 to 8
NEEDS   paper, your live pipeline in mind
STAGE   Steer           PART      Solo
SOURCE  The Cruz Protocol, Ch 16
```

## Open it

> "Fifteen minutes, no prep. This trains the asking label, which gets a
> question answered without asking it. First list the five questions
> you most want answered in your pipeline. Then, next to each, the
> uncomfortable column: what asking it directly would reveal about
> you. Go."

## Run it

1. Each person lists the **five questions they most want answered**
   across their live pipeline. The real ones. Budget, competition,
   authority, deadline, the actual obstacle.
2. Next to each, write **what asking it directly would reveal about
   you.** "Do you have budget?" reveals you fear there is none. "Who
   else are you talking to?" reveals you think you are losing. This
   column is the uncomfortable one, and it is the point of the drill.
3. Convert each question into an asking label from the shipped shapes.
   "It seems like there's a number in mind, even if it hasn't been
   said yet." "It seems like this needs a yes from someone else in the
   room first." "It sounds like you're comparing this against
   something else you're weighing." "It sounds like there's a reason
   this can't wait much longer." **No question marks allowed.**
4. Say all five out loud, and **hold six seconds after each.** The
   silence is half the tool, and it has to be drilled with the
   sentence, not added later.
5. Pick the deal where you have been avoiding a direct question for
   weeks and commit to the label version this week.

## Score it

Five conversions, binary each: no question mark, spoken or implied by
tone, and a genuine six-count held. Next session, one carried number:
did the committed label get said on the real deal, and what came back.

## What goes wrong

**Labels with a rising end.** The sentence is right and the tone
converts it back into a question at the last syllable. Same fix as the
Label Round: deliver it twice, flat and rising, and let the room hear
which one invites an answer and which one begs for it.

**Column two gets skipped as obvious.** It is not obvious. The
reliable discovery of this drill is that most reps have never once
considered that their questions are disclosures, and seeing five of
their own laid out that way changes how they open calls permanently.
Protect the column with four quiet minutes.

**The six seconds collapse in company.** People hold silence alone at
a desk and break it instantly with an audience. Run the out-loud round
in pairs, listener silent, so the six-count gets practiced against a
face.

## Harder

The listener answers the label with nothing but a mild "hm." The
operator must hold a second silence rather than downgrading to the
direct question. Two silences on one label is field-grade patience.

## Debrief

One question: "Which of your five questions was the biggest
disclosure?" The answers are usually the same two across the whole
room, budget and competition, and knowing that changes how the floor
talks about both.
