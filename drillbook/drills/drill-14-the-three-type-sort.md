# Drill 14: The Three-Type Sort

*Trains: decoding an attack's type before answering it, instead of
defending on reflex.*

```
TIME    15 min          PEOPLE    3 (attacker, negotiator, observer)
NEEDS   three cards marked DEFENSIVE, UNHEARD, TACTICAL
STAGE   Disarm          PART      The room
SOURCE  The Cruz Protocol, Ch 14
```

## Open it

> "Fifteen minutes, threes. This trains what you do in the two seconds
> after someone comes at you, which is decode before you defend. An
> attack is defensive fear, unheard frustration, or tactical pressure,
> and the wrong read gets the wrong response. Attackers, draw your card
> and stay in character. Negotiators, decoder line first, then silence.
> Go."

## Run it

1. One attacker, one negotiator, one observer. The attacker draws a
   card: **DEFENSIVE, UNHEARD, or TACTICAL.** The negotiator does not
   see it.
2. The attacker opens hot, in character, and stays in character for
   **ninety seconds.** A defensive attacker is scared of something and
   covering it with volume. An unheard one has said the same thing
   three times and nobody moved. A tactical one is applying pressure on
   purpose and watching for effect.
3. The negotiator's only job in the storm: **one decoder line from
   Chapter 14, then silence.** Not a defense. Not a correction. The
   decoder line and the quiet are what buy the read.
4. Then one response line **matched to the type they read.** Reassure
   the defensive. Prove hearing to the unheard. Stay level and
   unmoved with the tactical.
5. The observer records two things only: did the negotiator label
   before defending, yes or no, and did they call the type correctly,
   yes or no. Rotate. Three rounds, everyone attacks once.

`[NEEDS: three real opening attack lines from Dan's actual sessions.
One per type, to replace generic roleplay prompts. Invented attacks
are too polite and the drill runs soft without them.]`

## Score it

The observer's two binaries, tallied across rounds on the board.
Labeled-before-defended is the discipline number. Type-called-right is
the diagnostic number. Track them separately, because they fail
separately and get coached differently.

## What goes wrong

**The negotiator pre-diagnoses.** They decide the type from the first
five words and commit. Run the first full round type-unknown before
anyone attempts to guess early. Reps who guess before labeling get it
right at roughly chance and then commit to the wrong response, which
is worse than not decoding at all.

**The attacker breaks character.** They soften mid-round because
attacking a colleague feels rude. The observer restarts the clock. A
polite attack teaches nothing, and the field is not polite.

**The defense leaks in sideways.** "I hear you, and just to clarify
what we actually said" is a defense wearing a decoder's coat. The
observer scores it as defended, and says so in the tally.

## Harder

The attacker draws two cards across the round, switching type at the
forty-five second mark. Real counterparts do exactly this, a tactical
open that turns genuinely unheard when the script stops working. The
negotiator must show a visible re-read.

## Debrief

One question: "What did the attacker do that told you the type?" The
answers build the room's shared tell list, which is the asset this
drill exists to compound.
