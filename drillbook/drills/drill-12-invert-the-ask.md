# Drill 12: Invert the Ask

*Trains: rewriting yes-chasing questions into no-oriented ones you would
actually say.*

```
TIME    15 min          PEOPLE    1 solo, or 3 to 8
NEEDS   paper, this week's real conversations fresh in memory
STAGE   Close           PART      Solo
SOURCE  The Cruz Protocol, Ch 12
```

## Open it

> "Fifteen minutes, no prep. This trains the no-oriented question, which
> gives people a no to say instead of chasing a yes they'll resent.
> Write ten questions you actually asked this week. Real ones, word for
> word. Then we sort them. Go."

## Run it

1. Each person writes **ten questions they actually asked this week.**
   Real ones, from real conversations, verbatim. Not questions they wish
   they had asked. The embarrassment of the real list is the raw
   material.
2. Mark each one **yes-oriented** or **no-oriented.** A yes-oriented
   question chases agreement. A no-oriented one makes "no" the easy,
   safe answer. Most people discover nine or ten of theirs chase yes.
3. Rewrite every yes-oriented question as a no-oriented one. "Can we
   talk budget?" becomes "Would it be crazy to talk budget today?" "Are
   you still interested?" becomes "Have you given up on this?"
4. Say all ten out loud, around the room or alone at a desk. They will
   sound wrong. That is the point. The phrasing is unfamiliar, not
   incorrect, and the discomfort fades at about the sixth repetition.
5. Pick two and commit, out loud, to using them tomorrow.

## Score it

Two numbers. The yes-to-no ratio on the original list, which is the
baseline, and next session, how many of the two committed questions
actually got asked. The second number is the only one that compounds.

## What goes wrong

**The rewrites go manipulative.** "You don't want to lose this deal, do
you?" is not a no-oriented question. It is a corner. The test: a real
"no" answer must be genuinely fine with you. If a no would sting, the
question is still chasing yes with different furniture.

**People write the questions they should have asked.** The list goes
aspirational and the sort finds nothing to fix. Insist on verbatim
recall, and let the room hear that everyone's real list was nine to one.
The shared embarrassment is what makes the rewrite stick.

**The out-loud step gets skipped as childish.** It is the drill. A
rewritten question that has never been in your mouth will not come out
of it under pressure. Six repetitions is where the strangeness dies.

## Harder

Take the single most important question in your current pipeline, the
one you have been avoiding, and write its no-oriented form. Most people
find the no-oriented version is one they could actually say out loud,
which is the difference between a question that exists and a question
that gets asked.

## Debrief

One question: "Which rewrite could you say tomorrow morning without
rehearsing?" Commitment to one real question beats admiration of ten.
