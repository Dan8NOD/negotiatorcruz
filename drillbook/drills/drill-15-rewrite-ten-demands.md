# Drill 15: Rewrite Ten Demands

*Trains: converting demands into How and What questions, with a
follow-up ready for "there's no way."*

```
TIME    20 min          PEOPLE    1 solo, or 3 to 8
NEEDS   paper, this week's real demands fresh in memory
STAGE   Steer           PART      Solo
SOURCE  The Cruz Protocol, Ch 15
```

## Open it

> "Twenty minutes, works alone. This trains the calibrated question,
> which hands your problem to the other side inside a How or a What.
> Write ten demands you actually made this week. Word for word,
> including the ones that worked. Go."

## Run it

1. Each person writes **ten demands they actually made this week.**
   Real ones, verbatim. "We need the signed order by Friday." "You'll
   have to come up on the price." Demands made at home count, and they
   are usually the most honest entries on the list.
2. Rewrite each as a **How or What question** in the shipped grammar:
   "How am I supposed to do that?" "What would need to be true for
   this to work?" "How should we split this up so nothing falls
   through the cracks?" "What would need to happen for this to be an
   easy yes?"
3. Read all ten out loud. They will feel unnatural, indirect, and
   slightly weak. That feeling is the trained instinct objecting, and
   it is wrong. The question outperforms the demand in every measured
   category except how decisive it makes you feel.
4. Then the hard part, and the step people skip: **for each question,
   write what you would do if they answered "nothing, there's no
   way."** A calibrated question you have no follow-up for is a
   question you will end up answering yourself, out loud, badly.

## Score it

Ten rewrites, ten follow-ups, binary per line. The score that matters
is the follow-up column. A sheet with ten elegant questions and three
follow-ups is a sheet that fails in the field, and it should score
like one.

## What goes wrong

**Demands wearing question marks.** "How about you just sign it
today?" has rewritten nothing. The test is who ends up holding the
problem. If the question tells them your answer, it is still a demand.

**The list goes soft.** People pick their gentlest demands to rewrite
so the exercise flatters them. Require at least three entries they
are not proud of. The rewrite only proves itself against the demands
that felt justified.

**Step four becomes a discussion.** The follow-ups must be written,
per line, before anyone talks. Spoken follow-ups evaporate. Written
ones survive to the next real call.

## Harder

Trade sheets. Each person answers a neighbor's calibrated question
with a flat "nothing, there's no way," and the author must run their
written follow-up out loud, cold. The gap between the follow-up on
paper and the follow-up in the mouth is the last mile of this tool.

## Debrief

One question: "Which demand, as a question, would have changed a real
outcome this week?" One concrete answer per person. The drill earns
its twenty minutes on that single line.
