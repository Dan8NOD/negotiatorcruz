# Drill 13: Validate, Don't Concede

*Trains: validating an objection you disagree with, without giving
anything and without "but."*

```
TIME    20 min          PEOPLE    3 per group (operator, counterpart, listener)
NEEDS   real objections from live pipeline
STAGE   Disarm          PART     Pairs, with a listener
SOURCE  The Cruz Protocol, Ch 13
```

## Open it

> "Twenty minutes, threes. This trains validating something you
> disagree with while conceding nothing, which most people think is a
> contradiction. It isn't, and that's the drill. Listeners, you have
> one job: catch the word 'but.' One 'but' ends the round. Go."

## Run it

1. The counterpart states an objection the operator **genuinely
   disagrees with.** Real ones from real pipeline, said with some
   conviction.
2. The operator must validate it and **concede nothing.** An empathy
   statement or a positive label from the shipped catalog: "It makes
   sense you'd want more than my word on this." "That reaction makes
   complete sense given what's on the line." "It seems like you'd like
   to make this work, but there are things in the way." No movement on
   terms, no "we could look at," no softening of the number.
3. The listener listens for one thing only: **the word "but."** Any
   "but," "however," or "that said" immediately following the
   validation ends the round on the spot. Restart with a fresh
   objection.
4. Second pass: the operator validates, adds **one trust-repair
   question**, "What would proof look like here, if my word alone
   isn't enough?", and then stops talking entirely.
5. Rotate all three seats.

## Score it

Rounds survived without a "but," out of rounds attempted. Then the
counterpart reports one thing: did the validation feel like agreement?
The goal is no. Validation that reads as concession is the failure this
drill exists to separate out.

## What goes wrong

**The delayed but.** The operator validates cleanly, breathes, and
detonates it four words later. "I hear you. Completely fair. The thing
is though." The listener's mandate covers the whole turn, not just the
first sentence. Almost everyone fails this way first, because the
validation feels like a debt that has to be immediately balanced. It
is not a debt. You can validate and simply be quiet.

**Validation by agreement.** "You're right, it is expensive" has
conceded the frame. The shipped lines validate the person's position
and feeling, never the claim's correctness. Put one of each on the
board so the room can hear the difference.

**Tone that files a complaint.** A validation delivered with a sigh is
a rebuttal. If the counterpart laughs, rerun it flat.

## Harder

The counterpart escalates after the validation: "so you agree, then,
we should get a discount." The operator must hold the line with a
second validation and still concede nothing. Two clean validations
under pressure, zero movement, is Level 2 work.

## Debrief

One question: "What did you want to say instead of nothing?" Naming
the suppressed rebuttal out loud is how the room learns the quiet was
a choice, and that it worked.
