# How to Run a Drill

*Six pages, and the part of this book you will reread. Everything after it is
forty variations on what is here.*

---

## The whole method in one line

**Set it up in under a minute, run it slightly too fast, score it with
numbers, and stop while people still want another round.**

Almost every failed practice session in a business setting fails on one of
those four, and usually the last one.

---

## Before anyone arrives

**Pick the drill from the problem, not the calendar.** Open the index of what
is broken. Run the drill that fixes the thing your floor is currently worst
at, and keep running it until the number moves. A different drill every week
is entertainment. The same drill four weeks running is training.

**Have the material ready.** If the drill needs a recording, it is queued
before people sit down. A facilitator hunting for a file is a facilitator
telling the room this is optional.

**Decide what you are scoring, and write it on the board.** One number.
Talk-time percentage, seconds of silence held, labels that landed. If the room
does not know what is being counted, the round produces opinions, and opinions
are the failure state of practice.

---

## The first sixty seconds

The opening sets whether people take it seriously, and most facilitators talk
for four minutes when they have one.

**Say what it trains, say how long, say what gets scored, start.** In that
order, no preamble about the importance of negotiation.

> "This is fifteen minutes. It trains saying the label out loud, which is
> different from knowing what the label is. We're counting one thing: whether
> the sentence starts with 'I.' Pairs. Go."

**Do not explain the theory first.** It is tempting because it is comfortable
and because you know it well. The room learns more from doing it badly for
ninety seconds and then hearing the correction than from any amount of setup.
Explain after the first round, when they have something to attach it to.

**Never demonstrate it well first.** If you must demo, demo it **badly**, then
correctly. A polished demonstration sets a bar people quietly decide they
cannot reach, and the room disengages before it starts. A bad demo gets a
laugh and permission to be bad, which is the actual precondition for practice.

---

## While it runs

**Run it fifteen percent too fast.** Slightly rushed keeps people in
performance rather than analysis, which is where the skill lives. If everyone
finishes comfortably, the timing was wrong.

**Do not rescue anyone.** The single most common facilitator error. Someone
stalls, the room goes quiet, and the instinct is to step in with the line they
were reaching for. Let it sit. The four seconds of silence a struggling rep
sits in is the same four seconds the drill exists to train, and rescuing them
teaches the room that discomfort will be removed if they wait.

**Watch, and write down what you see, in numbers.** Not "good job on that
one." *"Four seconds."* *"Started with I, twice."* *"Sixty-eight percent."*

**Let people be bad at it out loud.** The first attempt at any of these is
worse than the person's untrained behavior, because deliberate execution is
harder than habit. Say that in advance and the room stops treating the dip as
evidence the method is wrong.

---

## Scoring

**Numbers, not impressions.** Every drill in this book has a scored line, and
every scored line is countable off a recording or a stopwatch by a person with
no negotiation training. That is deliberate. A score anyone can verify is a
score nobody argues with.

**Score the behavior, never the person.** *"Row 7, no."* Not *"you're
defensive."* The first is a fact about ninety seconds of tape. The second is a
claim about someone's character, and it ends tape submission on the whole
floor within about a fortnight.

**One correction per person per session.** Not three, not the full list.
Someone leaving with four things to fix implements zero, and you will have
spent your credibility to achieve nothing.

---

## The debrief

Two minutes. Not ten.

**Ask something answerable.** *"How did that feel"* produces politeness.
Instead: *"At what second did you want to fill the silence?"* *"Which of your
three emotions had no evidence?"* *"Who spoke first?"* A question with a
factual answer produces a factual answer, and factual answers are what people
remember on Tuesday.

**Name one thing the room did well, specifically.** Not encouragement, data.
People generally do not know which of their instincts are good, so a
specifically named strength gets repeated and a general compliment does not.

**End on the commitment.** One behavior, one week, one check. *"Next two
calls, label before you name a number. I'll pull two tapes Friday."*

Then stop. **Stop while they still want another round.** A session that ends
five minutes early gets remembered as short and useful. One that runs five
minutes long gets remembered as long, and the next one has three people
missing.

---

## When it goes flat

It will. Three usual causes, in the order to check them.

**Nobody is being scored.** Practice without a number degenerates into
conversation inside about six minutes. Restore the count and the energy comes
back.

**The scenario is invented.** People cannot roleplay a deal they do not have.
Every drill in this book that involves a scenario says *use a real one from
your own pipeline*, and that instruction is load-bearing rather than
decorative. Invented objections are too polite, they collapse the moment
anyone pushes, and the room learns that the tools work on straw.

**The room does not believe it is safe.** If being bad at a drill in front of
a manager has consequences, nobody will be bad at it, which means nobody will
be practicing. You cannot instruct your way out of this. You fix it by being
visibly bad at one yourself, first, in front of them.

Do that once, genuinely, at the start of any rollout. It costs you nothing you
were actually using and it buys the only condition under which any of this
works.

---

## What this is not

**It is not a workshop.** No slides. Nothing here needs a projector.

**It is not team building.** Nobody is going to enjoy the first three weeks
and you should not pretend otherwise. The enjoyment arrives at about week
four, when a tool works on a real call and the rep tells the room about it
unprompted. That moment is the actual product of this book.

**It is not a substitute for negotiating.** The drills compress the reps you
would otherwise get in three years of expensive deals. They do not replace the
deals.
